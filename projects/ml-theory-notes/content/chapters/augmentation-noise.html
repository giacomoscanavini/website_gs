Augmentation, invariance, and noise injection

Augmentation encodes which changes should preserve a target. Noise injection encourages robustness to perturbations and, for linear regression with squared loss, has an exact connection to L2 regularization.

Augmentation encodes a statement about the target

Let TT be a sampled transformation. The augmented objective is

Laug(θ)=1n∑i𝔼T[ℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))].L_{\mathrm{aug}}(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb E_T\left[\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i))\right].

The crucial assumption is that the chosen transformation preserves the relevant target. If y(Tx)=y(x)y(Tx)=y(x) , training on both variants encourages an invariance such as fθ(Tx)≈fθ(x)f_\theta(Tx)\approx f_\theta(x) .

If a transformation changes the target, retaining the original label creates mislabeled data. This is a concern for orientation-sensitive images, medical anatomy, text, and structurally constrained tabular data.

Step by step Derive the augmented objective and its stochastic gradient

Setting. A transformation TT is drawn from a fixed distribution qq independently of the parameters. For classification-style invariance, assume the label remains valid: y(Tx)=y(x)y(Tx)=y(x) . For segmentation or other equivariant tasks, both input and target must instead transform consistently.

1 · Start with the loss for a single transformed example

For observation ii , the loss is ℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i)) . Since training can see different transformations of the same example, its expected contribution is the integral

𝔼T[ℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))]=∫ℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))q(dT).\mathbb E_T[\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i))]=\int\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i))q(dT).

2 · Average over the observed sample

Jaug(θ)=1n∑i=1n𝔼T[ℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))].J_{\mathrm{aug}}(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbb E_T[\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i))].

A single random transform per selected observation is a Monte Carlo estimate of this expectation. It does not make the augmented observations statistically independent new labels.

3 · Obtain a stochastic gradient under explicit assumptions

Assume differentiation may pass under the integral, for example through a locally integrable dominating bound on the derivative. Then

∇Jaug(θ)=1n∑i𝔼T[∇θℓ(yi,fθ(Txi))].\nabla J_{\mathrm{aug}}(\theta)=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbb E_T[\nabla_\theta\ell(y_i,f_\theta(Tx_i))].

If II is a fresh uniform index and T∼qT\sim q , then 𝔼I,T[∇θℓ(yI,fθ(TxI))]=∇Jaug(θ)\mathbb E_{I,T}[\nabla_\theta\ell(y_I,f_\theta(Tx_I))]=\nabla J_{\mathrm{aug}}(\theta) . A transformation policy learned jointly with the model requires differentiating its parameter dependence too; this argument holds qq fixed.

4 · Separate encouragement from exact invariance

The objective rewards low loss over sampled variations, but does not algebraically enforce fθ(Tx)=fθ(x)f_\theta(Tx)=f_\theta(x) . For an exact finite-group construction, define f‾(x)=|G|−1∑g∈Gf(gx)\bar f(x)=|G|^{-1}\sum_{g\in G}f(gx) . For any h∈Gh\in G , the products ghgh enumerate GG again, so

f‾(hx)=1|G|∑g∈Gf(ghx)=1|G|∑u∈Gf(ux)=f‾(x).\bar f(hx)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{g\in G}f(ghx)=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{u\in G}f(ux)=\bar f(x).

Exact group averaging provides invariance by construction. Random augmentation merely encourages related behavior through optimization and finite samples.

Different data types allow different invariances

Small translations or some crops may preserve an image category. Audio perturbations may model nuisance changes in acquisition. Text transformations can alter meaning even when only a few tokens change. Financial or other temporal tables may have constraints that arbitrary row shuffling, time reversal, or independent feature noise destroys.

Exact duplication is not the same as useful augmentation. Duplicating one observation changes its weight in the objective; duplicating every observation equally leaves a mean objective unchanged. A valid transformation adds a preference about how nearby or equivalent inputs should be treated.

Before adopting an image transformation, inspect the original and transformed inputs together. Cropping, rotation, or color changes are useful only when the resulting target remains appropriate.

Noise injection emphasizes local robustness

For Gaussian input noise,

x̃=x+ϵ,ϵ∼𝒩(0,σ2I),\tilde x=x+\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I),

and the training objective averages the loss over perturbations. If a small input change causes a large deterioration, that model is penalized by the noisy objective. A Taylor expansion relates the effect to derivatives with respect to the input.

Noise can act on inputs, activations, weights, gradients, or labels. These are different operations: input noise changes observed examples, while gradient noise changes the update dynamics. Label perturbations require particular care because they change the supervised target.

Derive the exact linear-regression identity

For fβ(x)=x⊤βf_\beta(x)=x^\top\beta , perturb the design by an independent matrix EE with entries of mean zero and variance σ2\sigma^2 . Let r=y−Xβr=y-X\beta . Then

∥y−(X+E)β∥2=∥r∥2−2r⊤Eβ+∥Eβ∥2.\lVert y-(X+E)\beta\rVert^2 =\lVert r\rVert^2-2r^\top E\beta+\lVert E\beta\rVert^2.

The expected cross term is zero. Independence and isotropic coordinate variance give 𝔼[E⊤E]=nσ2I\mathbb E[E^\top E]=n\sigma^2I , so

𝔼∥Eβ∥2=nσ2∥β∥2.\mathbb E\lVert E\beta\rVert^2=n\sigma^2\lVert\beta\rVert^2.

Therefore

𝔼E∥y−(X+E)β∥2=∥y−Xβ∥2+nσ2∥β∥2.\mathbb E_E\lVert y-(X+E)\beta\rVert^2 =\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2+n\sigma^2\lVert\beta\rVert^2.

Under the stated summed-loss convention, the extra term is exactly an L2 penalty. Dividing the whole objective by nn also divides the penalty scale. This is why normalization must remain explicit.

Step by step Expand the exact noise–L2 identity, then identify the nonlinear remainder

Setting. Hold X,y,βX,y,\beta fixed. Let the nn noise rows εi⊤\varepsilon_i^\top have mean zero and covariance Σε\Sigma_\varepsilon . Put them into EE , so noisy predictions are (X+E)β(X+E)\beta . Gaussianity is not needed for the quadratic expectation; the stated first two moments suffice.

1 · Expand the residual norm before taking expectations

Let r=y−Xβr=y-X\beta . Then

∥r−Eβ∥2=r⊤r−2r⊤Eβ+β⊤E⊤Eβ.\|r-E\beta\|^2=r^\top r-2r^\top E\beta+\beta^\top E^\top E\beta.

Since 𝔼[E]=0\mathbb E[E]=0 , the middle term has expectation zero.

2 · Evaluate the remaining random term row by row

∥Eβ∥2=∑i=1n(εi⊤β)2=∑iβ⊤εiεi⊤β.\|E\beta\|^2=\sum_{i=1}^n(\varepsilon_i^\top\beta)^2 =\sum_i\beta^\top\varepsilon_i\varepsilon_i^\top\beta.

Taking expectations gives 𝔼∥Eβ∥2=nβ⊤Σεβ\mathbb E\|E\beta\|^2=n\beta^\top\Sigma_\varepsilon\beta . Therefore

𝔼∥y−(X+E)β∥2=∥y−Xβ∥2+nβ⊤Σεβ.\mathbb E\|y-(X+E)\beta\|^2=\|y-X\beta\|^2+n\beta^\top\Sigma_\varepsilon\beta.

For isotropic covariance Σε=σ2I\Sigma_\varepsilon=\sigma^2I, the extra term is an nσ2∥β∥2n\sigma^2\|\beta\|^2 penalty. For mean half-squared loss, divide every term by 2n2n, giving penalty σ2∥β∥2/2\sigma^2\|\beta\|^2/2.

3 · See what changes for a nonlinear predictor

For one input, use loss L(x)=12[f(x)−y]2L(x)=\frac12[f(x)-y]^2 , residual r=f(x)−yr=f(x)-y , gradient a=∇xf(x)a=\nabla_xf(x) , and Hessian HfH_f . A second-order expansion is

f(x+ε)−y=r+a⊤ε+12ε⊤Hfε+higher-order terms.f(x+\varepsilon)-y=r+a^\top\varepsilon+\tfrac12\varepsilon^\top H_f\varepsilon+\text{higher-order terms}.

Squaring and keeping terms through second order in the noise gives

L(x+ε)≈12r2+ra⊤ε+12(a⊤ε)2+r2ε⊤Hfε.L(x+\varepsilon)\approx\frac12r^2+r a^\top\varepsilon +\frac12(a^\top\varepsilon)^2+\frac r2\varepsilon^\top H_f\varepsilon.

Terms such as (a⊤ε)(ε⊤Hfε)(a^\top\varepsilon)(\varepsilon^\top H_f\varepsilon) are third order; the squared quadratic term is fourth order.

4 · Take the second-order expectation

Using 𝔼ε=0\mathbb E\varepsilon=0 and 𝔼[ε⊤Aε]=tr(AΣε)\mathbb E[\varepsilon^\top A\varepsilon]=\operatorname{tr}(A\Sigma_\varepsilon) ,

𝔼[L(x+ε)]≈L(x)+12a⊤Σεa+r2tr(HfΣε).\mathbb E[L(x+\varepsilon)]\approx L(x) +\frac12a^\top\Sigma_\varepsilon a +\frac r2\operatorname{tr}(H_f\Sigma_\varepsilon).

For isotropic noise, the extra terms are σ2∥∇f∥2/2\sigma^2\|\nabla f\|^2/2 and σ2rtr(Hf)/2\sigma^2r\operatorname{tr}(H_f)/2 . The second term need not be nonnegative. Thus a nonlinear noisy objective is not generally exactly Ridge, nor solely a gradient-norm penalty. The expansion needs sufficiently small noise and smoothness; the linear identity above is exact.

Boundaries of the connection

The exact identity uses linear prediction, squared error, and the stated independent noise model. A nonlinear network’s response includes higher-order effects, and anisotropic noise gives a different quadratic form. For broader models, a local Taylor expansion describes additional derivative terms; it does not establish an exact equivalence with ordinary Ridge.

Noise strength also has meaning only relative to feature scale. A single σ\sigma can be negligible for one column and overwhelming for another. A large perturbation can leave the region where the label-preservation assumption is plausible.

The Python example checks the linear noise–penalty identity by Monte Carlo averaging. Its approximation error comes from the finite number of noise samples, while the displayed identity concerns the exact expectation.

Check the regularization identities Local worked example · Python / PyTorch

Numerical checks of orthogonal Ridge, soft thresholding, and inverted dropout, plus a seeded Monte Carlo check of the Gaussian input-noise identity. Run the script locally.

regularization_identities.py 
"""Small numerical checks for regularization identities from the supplied guide"""

import torch


def main() -> None:
    torch.set_num_threads(1)
    generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(41)
    dtype = torch.float64

    # Orthogonal Ridge example with the source's unnormalized objective
    x = torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype)
    y = torch.tensor([3.0, -2.0, 0.5], dtype=dtype)
    ridge = torch.linalg.solve(x.T @ x + torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype), x.T @ y)
    torch.testing.assert_close(ridge, torch.tensor([1.5, -1.0, 0.25], dtype=dtype))
    print("Orthogonal Ridge coefficients:", ridge.tolist())

    # The LASSO soft threshold is exact for this orthogonal scalar subproblem
    z = torch.tensor([2.4, 0.5, -1.1], dtype=dtype)
    lasso = z.sign() * (z.abs() - 0.7).clamp_min(0)
    torch.testing.assert_close(lasso, torch.tensor([1.7, 0.0, -0.4], dtype=dtype))
    print("Soft-thresholded coefficients:", lasso.tolist())

    # Verify the Gaussian input-noise identity approximately by Monte Carlo
    x = torch.randn(5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    beta = torch.tensor([0.6, -0.3, 0.8], dtype=dtype)
    y = x @ beta + torch.tensor([0.1, -0.2, 0.3, -0.1, 0.2], dtype=dtype)
    sigma = 0.2
    perturbations = sigma * torch.randn(100_000, 5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    noisy_losses = (y - (x + perturbations) @ beta).square().sum(dim=1)
    empirical = noisy_losses.mean()
    analytical = (y - x @ beta).square().sum() + len(x) * sigma**2 * beta.square().sum()
    standard_error = noisy_losses.std() / noisy_losses.numel()**0.5
    assert (empirical - analytical).abs() < 6 * standard_error
    print(f"Expected noisy squared loss: {analytical.item():.6f}")
    print(f"Monte Carlo estimate: {empirical.item():.6f} ± {standard_error.item():.6f} SE")

    # Reproduce the fixed-mask inverted-dropout example
    h = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, -3.0, 4.0], dtype=dtype)
    mask = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype)
    masked = mask * h / 0.5
    torch.testing.assert_close(masked, torch.tensor([4.0, 0.0, -6.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype))
    print("Inverted dropout under the illustrated mask:", masked.tolist())
    print("Exact checks passed; the noise expectation is a finite-sample approximation")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Check your understanding When is Gaussian input noise equivalent to L2 regularization?

For linear predictions, squared error, and independent zero-mean isotropic feature noise, the expected noisy squared error equals the original squared error plus nσ²‖β‖² under the summed-loss convention. The identity is not an exact universal equivalence for nonlinear models or arbitrary noise.
