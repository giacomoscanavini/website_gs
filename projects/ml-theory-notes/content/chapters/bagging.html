Bootstrap aggregating

Bagging trains models on perturbed versions of the dataset and combines their predictions. The guide’s ordinary bootstrap draws nn observations with replacement from nn originals. Some rows appear repeatedly; others are absent.

The probability that a particular row is omitted is

(1−1n)n→e−1≈0.368.\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\longrightarrow e^{-1}\approx0.368.

Thus a bootstrap dataset of size nn contains about 63.2%63.2\% of the original observations at least once in the large- nn limit. Size and number of unique rows are different quantities.

For regression, average the predictions. For classification, the guide uses class-probability averaging or majority voting. The combination is a separate stage from fitting each component.

Setting. Draw nn indices independently and uniformly with replacement from nn original observations. This is the ordinary size- nn bootstrap, not sampling without replacement.

Exponentiating gives P→e−1≈0.367879P\to e^{-1}\approx0.367879 . For finite nn , the exact formula, not e−1e^{-1} , is the probability. At n=1n=1 it is zero.

3 · Count unique observations with indicators

Let Ii=1I_i=1 if original observation ii appears at least once. The unique count is U=∑iIiU=\sum_iI_i . Linearity of expectation does not require the indicators to be independent:

𝔼[U]=∑i𝔼[Ii]=n[1−(1−1n)n].\mathbb E[U]=\sum_i\mathbb E[I_i]=n\left[1-\left(1-\frac1n\right)^n\right].

Thus the expected included fraction tends to 1−e−1≈0.6321211-e^{-1}\approx0.632121 , even though the sample contains exactly nn draws.

4 · Interpret out-of-bag predictions with their training boundary

For each observation, aggregate only models whose bootstrap training sample omitted it. This yields an out-of-bag prediction under the ensemble construction. It does not automatically protect against preprocessing fitted using that observation, dependent entities, or future information. A temporal block bootstrap preserves some local dependence but does not by itself turn an OOB estimate into a chronological forecasting test.

Derive the variance of an average

At a fixed input, assume the fitted predictions have common variance σ2\sigma^2 and pairwise correlation ρ\rho over fitting randomness. The same covariance applies to errors relative to the fixed conditional mean, excluding fresh shared observation noise. Then

Var(1M∑mf̂m)=1M2[Mσ2+M(M−1)ρσ2].\operatorname{Var}\left(\frac1M\sum_m\hat f_m\right) =\frac1{M^2}\left[M\sigma^2+M(M-1)\rho\sigma^2\right].

There are MM variance terms and M(M−1)M(M-1) ordered covariance terms. Simplifying,

Var(f‾)=σ2[ρ+1−ρM].\operatorname{Var}(\bar f)=\sigma^2\left[\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\right].

At zero correlation, the familiar 1/M1/M reduction appears. At perfect positive correlation, the variance does not fall at all. With a fixed positive common correlation, the limit is ρσ2\rho\sigma^2 .

The equal-variance/equal-correlation assumptions make this a clean explanatory model, not a literal formula for every ensemble. It explains why useful diversity of errors matters more than the number of differently named models.

Step by step Expand every covariance term in the variance of an average

Setting. At a fixed input, let F1,…,FMF_1,\ldots,F_M be predictions varying over model fitting. Assume all have variance σ2\sigma^2 and distinct pairs have covariance ρσ2\rho\sigma^2 . Equivalently, use errors relative to the fixed true conditional mean. Do not add an independent new-outcome noise term to each error and then count that shared noise as reducible model variance.

1 · Center the average

Let F‾=M−1∑mFm\bar F=M^{-1}\sum_mF_m and μm=𝔼[Fm]\mu_m=\mathbb E[F_m] . Then

F‾−𝔼[F‾]=1M∑m(Fm−μm).\bar F-\mathbb E[\bar F]=\frac1M\sum_m(F_m-\mu_m).

2 · Expand the square as a double sum

Var(F‾)=1M2∑i=1M∑j=1M𝔼[(Fi−μi)(Fj−μj)].\operatorname{Var}(\bar F)=\frac1{M^2}\sum_{i=1}^M\sum_{j=1}^M \mathbb E[(F_i-\mu_i)(F_j-\mu_j)].

There are MM diagonal terms with i=ji=j , each equal to σ2\sigma^2 . There are M(M−1)M(M-1) ordered off-diagonal terms, each equal to ρσ2\rho\sigma^2 . Counting ordered pairs is why no extra factor of two is needed.

3 · Simplify the sum

Var(F‾)=Mσ2+M(M−1)ρσ2M2=σ2[ρ+1−ρM].\operatorname{Var}(\bar F)=\frac{M\sigma^2+M(M-1)\rho\sigma^2}{M^2} =\sigma^2\left[\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\right].

If ρ=0\rho=0 , variance is σ2/M\sigma^2/M . If ρ=1\rho=1 , averaging changes nothing. For M=10M=10 and ρ=0.2\rho=0.2 , it is σ2(0.2+0.8/10)=0.28σ2\sigma^2(0.2+0.8/10)=0.28\sigma^2 .

4 · Check which correlations are possible

The equicorrelation matrix is (1−ρ)I+ρ𝟏𝟏⊤(1-\rho)I+\rho\mathbf1\mathbf1^\top . Along 𝟏\mathbf1 its eigenvalue is 1+(M−1)ρ1+(M-1)\rho ; in directions orthogonal to 𝟏\mathbf1 it is 1−ρ1-\rho . A covariance matrix must be positive semidefinite, so for M>1M>1 ,

−1M−1≤ρ≤1.-\frac1{M-1}\le\rho\le1.

A fixed negative correlation cannot persist unchanged as MM grows without bound. The often-quoted nonzero limiting floor assumes a feasible fixed nonnegative ρ\rho .

5 · Return to prediction error

Variance reduction is only one term of squared prediction risk. Biased models can retain shared bias after averaging, and independent future observation noise remains irreducible. Diversity is useful when it lowers the relevant error covariance without making the component predictions excessively poor.

Why unstable trees benefit

The guide describes deep decision trees as high-variance estimators: small sample changes can alter early splits and therefore many downstream predictions. Bagging can average part of that instability away.

A random forest adds feature randomization when considering each split. That makes it harder for every tree to repeatedly choose the same strongest feature and can reduce correlation among trees. The motivation follows directly from the variance formula. Randomness that lowers useful signal too much would not be an improvement; the bias and diversity effects must be considered together.

This chapter follows the guide’s ensemble perspective on trees. It is not intended as a complete derivation of all tree-splitting algorithms.

Out-of-bag predictions

For observation ii , use only models whose bootstrap samples omitted it:

ŷiOOB=1|Mi|∑m∈Mif̂m(xi).\hat y_i^{\mathrm{OOB}}=\frac1{|M_i|}\sum_{m\in M_i}\hat f_m(x_i).

Each observation is evaluated by models that did not train on it. This can supply an internal generalization estimate for ordinary bootstrap ensembles in appropriate IID settings. The number of available out-of-bag models differs by observation; with very few models, some observations may have poor coverage.

OOB evaluation is not automatically equivalent to an untouched final test after unrestricted model selection. It is a measurement within a particular resampling procedure.

Why temporal data need different reasoning

Ordinary row-wise resampling assumes observations are exchangeable enough for that procedure. Resampling a time series independently can destroy dependence and allow future regimes to influence models used to score earlier observations. The source discusses time-aware procedures such as block/stationary bootstrap or explicitly ordered ensembles. Preserving blocks addresses some dependence, but does not by itself enforce forecasting chronology: evaluation must still prevent future information from entering a model used to predict an earlier outcome.

The official scikit-learn example already trains and plots a single estimator against bagging with a bias–variance decomposition. It is the relevant classical-ML implementation here; no additional PyTorch forest implementation is needed to make the theoretical point.

Check your understanding Why does adding more highly correlated models give diminishing returns?

With equal model variance σ² and common error correlation ρ, the average has variance σ²[ρ+(1−ρ)/M]. Increasing M reduces the independent component, but positive shared error leaves a floor ρσ². More models do not remove what they all get wrong together.
