Learning from consequences rather than labels

A supervised learner is given target answers. A reinforcement-learning agent chooses an action, receives a reward and a new observation, and learns a policy for choosing future actions. An action can change not only the next reward but also which situations the agent encounters later. The central problem is therefore sequential decision-making , not predicting a supplied correct action.

A robot can take a longer route to avoid a collision; a controller can accept a short-term cost to reach a more useful state. These are illustrative cases, not benchmarks. A neural network is optional: a small problem can use a table, while deep reinforcement learning uses neural networks to represent policies, value functions, or a model of the environment. A bandit is a useful simpler case where actions do not influence future decision states.

In a fully observed Markov decision process (MDP) , the current state and action determine the distribution of the next state and reward; earlier history contributes no additional predictive information once they are known. An observation need not be a complete state. With partial observations, history, a belief state, or a recurrent representation may be needed—the connection to the RNN chapter .

Returns, values, and the Bellman equation

For an episode with TT actions, the return from decision tt is

Gt=∑k=0T−t−1γkRt+k+1,GT=0.G_t=\sum_{k=0}^{T-t-1}\gamma^kR_{t+k+1},\qquad G_T=0.

This is a sum of rewards , not the immediate reward. With bounded rewards, 0≤γ<10\leq\gamma<1 also makes an infinite-horizon discounted return finite. A finite episode can use γ=1\gamma=1 . Discounting specifies the objective; it is not automatically a probability that the environment survives.

The state value Vπ(s)=𝔼π[Gt∣St=s]V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s] asks how good a state is under a particular policy . The action value Qπ(s,a)=𝔼π[Gt∣St=s,At=a]Q^\pi(s,a)=\mathbb E_\pi[G_t\mid S_t=s,A_t=a] asks about taking one specified action and following that policy afterward. Hence

Vπ(s)=∑aπ(a∣s)Qπ(s,a).V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)Q^\pi(s,a).

For a stationary discounted MDP, the Bellman expectation equation separates one reward from the remaining return:

Vπ(s)=∑aπ(a∣s)∑s′,rp(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γVπ(s′)].V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\bigl[r+\gamma V^\pi(s')\bigr].

Terminal states have zero future value; the reward for entering them still counts. In a finite-horizon task, value can depend on remaining time, so use VtπV_t^\pi or include time in the state. Averaging under π\pi evaluates a policy. Replacing a future policy average by a maximum defines an optimality equation; it is a different operation. Value functions and Bellman equations ↗

Step by step From a discounted return to the Bellman equations

Setting. An episode takes actions 0,…,T−10,\ldots,T-1 . Its final reward is RTR_T and GT=0G_T=0 . For the stationary Bellman form, use a stationary discounted MDP; for a finite-horizon example, include remaining time in the state. The following algebra is an authored expansion of the standard return and value definitions.

1 · Write out the discounted sum

Gt=Rt+1+γRt+2+γ2Rt+3+⋯+γT−t−1RT.G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma R_{t+2}+\gamma^2R_{t+3}+\cdots+\gamma^{T-t-1}R_T.

The first reward has exponent zero because it is the next reward from the present decision. The next return begins one decision later:

Gt+1=Rt+2+γRt+3+⋯+γT−t−2RT.G_{t+1}=R_{t+2}+\gamma R_{t+3}+\cdots+\gamma^{T-t-2}R_T.

2 · Factor the common discount

Every term after the first in GtG_t contains a factor γ\gamma . Factoring it leaves exactly Gt+1G_{t+1} :

Gt=Rt+1+γ[Rt+2+γRt+3+⋯]=Rt+1+γGt+1.G_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma\bigl[R_{t+2}+\gamma R_{t+3}+\cdots\bigr]=R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}.

At the last decision this gives GT−1=RT+γ⋅0=RTG_{T-1}=R_T+\gamma\cdot0=R_T . The same identity holds for a convergent infinite discounted sum.

3 · Condition on the present state

By definition and linearity of conditional expectation,

Vπ(s)=𝔼π[Rt+1+γGt+1∣St=s].V^\pi(s)=\mathbb E_\pi[R_{t+1}+\gamma G_{t+1}\mid S_t=s].

First condition on the action, then on the next state and reward. The action has probability π(a∣s)\pi(a\mid s) and the transition has probability p(s′,r∣s,a)p(s',r\mid s,a) . The Markov property and continuation under the same policy make the expected remaining return Vπ(s′)V^\pi(s') :

Vπ(s)=∑aπ(a∣s)∑s′,rp(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γVπ(s′)].V^\pi(s)=\sum_a\pi(a\mid s)\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\bigl[r+\gamma V^\pi(s')\bigr].

These are sums for discrete states/rewards; continuous quantities require integrals. For a finite horizon without time in the state, replace the left value by Vtπ(s)V_t^\pi(s) and the continuation by Vt+1π(s′)V_{t+1}^\pi(s') .

4 · Hold the first action fixed

If the first action is specified rather than sampled, remove the first policy average:

Qπ(s,a)=∑s′,rp(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γVπ(s′)].Q^\pi(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\bigl[r+\gamma V^\pi(s')\bigr].

Under an optimal policy, the best next-state value is maxa′Q*(s′,a′)\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a') , giving

Q*(s,a)=∑s′,rp(s′,r∣s,a)[r+γmaxa′Q*(s′,a′)].Q^*(s,a)=\sum_{s',r}p(s',r\mid s,a)\bigl[r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^*(s',a')\bigr].

The maximum stays inside the next-state expectation because the next action is chosen after observing the next state. It is not a maximum over the environment’s random outcomes.

5 · Work backward through a small choice

At s0s_0 , either collect reward 22 and stop, or wait through rewards (0,0,4)(0,0,4) before stopping. After choosing to wait, the two remaining actions are forced. With γ=0.9\gamma=0.9 ,

V(s2)=4,V(s1)=0+0.9(4)=3.6,V(s_2)=4,\qquad V(s_1)=0+0.9(4)=3.6,
Q(s0,wait)=0+0.9(3.6)=3.24,Q(s0,stop)=2.Q(s_0,\mathrm{wait})=0+0.9(3.6)=3.24,\qquad Q(s_0,\mathrm{stop})=2.

If the policy waits with probability p=0.5p=0.5 , its starting value is

Vπ(s0)=(1−p)2+p(4γ2)=0.5(2)+0.5(3.24)=2.62.V^\pi(s_0)=(1-p)2+p(4\gamma^2)=0.5(2)+0.5(3.24)=2.62.

The optimal action is wait when 4γ2>24\gamma^2>2 , so the threshold is γ>1/2≈0.7071\gamma>\sqrt{1/2}\approx0.7071 . Changing γ\gamma changes the preference even though neither path’s actual rewards change.

Learning values: Monte Carlo, TD, and Q-learning

An agent often does not know pp . It can learn from sampled transitions instead. Monte Carlo estimates a value using an observed return after an episode finishes. Temporal-difference (TD) learning uses an estimated future value to construct a target immediately; this is called bootstrapping . Here, bootstrapping is not the resampling procedure in the bagging chapter.

For estimating the value of the policy generating the data, TD(0) uses

δt=Rt+1+γ(1−dt)V(St+1)−V(St),\delta_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma(1-d_t)V(S_{t+1})-V(S_t),
V(St)←V(St)+αδt.V(S_t)\leftarrow V(S_t)+\alpha\delta_t.

Here dt=1d_t=1 only for a true terminal transition and α\alpha is the learning rate. Monte Carlo avoids a bootstrapped target but can have high variance; TD can update sooner, while errors in its current value estimate influence its target.

Q-learning instead moves toward a target containing the best estimated next action:

yt=Rt+1+γ(1−dt)maxa′Q(St+1,a′),y_t=R_{t+1}+\gamma(1-d_t)\max_{a'}Q(S_{t+1},a'),
Q(St,At)←Q(St,At)+α[yt−Q(St,At)].Q(S_t,A_t)\leftarrow Q(S_t,A_t)+\alpha\bigl[y_t-Q(S_t,A_t)\bigr].

It is off-policy : the behavior policy collecting experience can be exploratory even though the target uses a greedy next action. By contrast, SARSA uses the next action actually sampled under its behavior policy in the target. With ε\varepsilon -greedy exploration, choose a random action with probability ε\varepsilon and a greedy action otherwise. Exploitation uses what has been learned; exploration can discover better alternatives.

Step by step A Q-learning target and update, one calculation at a time

Setting. One observed transition is (s,a,r,s′,d)(s,a,r,s',d) , where dd records genuine termination. Use a scalar table entry q=Q(s,a)q=Q(s,a) and hold the target fixed during this update. This calculation describes a tabular Q-learning step and then its neural-network analogue; it is not a convergence proof.

1 · Form the continuation target

For a nonterminal transition, choose the largest estimated next-action value, then discount it and add the observed reward:

y=r+γ(1−d)maxa′Q(s′,a′).y=r+\gamma(1-d)\max_{a'}Q(s',a').

When d=1d=1 , the future term vanishes but the immediate reward remains. A sampled target is not the same as the exact Bellman expectation.

2 · Measure the prediction error

Define δ=y−q\delta=y-q . A positive δ\delta means the target is above the current estimate. Use a half-squared loss to make the derivative simple:

ℓ(q)=12(q−y)2.\ell(q)=\tfrac12(q-y)^2.

3 · Differentiate and take a descent step

The derivative of the square is twice its argument. The inner derivative is 11 because yy is fixed:

dℓdq=12⋅2(q−y)⋅1=q−y=−δ.\frac{d\ell}{dq}=\tfrac12\cdot2(q-y)\cdot1=q-y=-\delta.

Gradient descent subtracts this derivative:

qnew=q−α(q−y)=q+αδ=(1−α)q+αy.q_{\mathrm{new}}=q-\alpha(q-y)=q+\alpha\delta=(1-\alpha)q+\alpha y.

For 0≤α≤10\leq\alpha\leq1 , this is a weighted average of the old estimate and the target.

4 · Substitute actual values

Let q=1.5q=1.5 , r=1r=1 , γ=0.9\gamma=0.9 , maxa′Q(s′,a′)=3\max_{a'}Q(s',a')=3 , d=0d=0 , and α=0.2\alpha=0.2 :

y=1+0.9(3)=3.7,δ=3.7−1.5=2.2,y=1+0.9(3)=3.7,\qquad\delta=3.7-1.5=2.2,
qnew=1.5+0.2(2.2)=1.94.q_{\mathrm{new}}=1.5+0.2(2.2)=1.94.

For the otherwise identical terminal transition, y=1y=1 and qnew=1.5+0.2(1−1.5)=1.4q_{\mathrm{new}}=1.5+0.2(1-1.5)=1.4 . Confusing an artificial time cutoff with termination would therefore change the target and update.

5 · Replace the table entry with a network prediction

For network parameters θ\theta , the chain rule gives

∇θℓ=(Qθ(s,a)−y)∇θQθ(s,a).\nabla_\theta\ell=\bigl(Q_\theta(s,a)-y\bigr)\nabla_\theta Q_\theta(s,a).

A target network Qθ‾Q_{\bar\theta} forms yy with fixed parameters and disabled gradient tracking. The shared network update can change many state-action predictions at once, unlike updating one table entry. DQN often replaces the squared loss with Huber/Smooth L1 loss to limit the influence of large residuals. The official tutorial shows that implementation explicitly.

When the value table becomes a neural network

A Deep Q-Network (DQN) predicts one action value per discrete action. It regresses the selected Qθ(s,a)Q_\theta(s,a) toward a target computed without gradient tracking. Experience replay reuses transitions and reduces the correlation within training batches; a separate, more slowly updated target network reduces target movement. These stabilize training, but do not guarantee convergence of nonlinear Q-learning. Q-values are returns, not class probabilities: no output softmax is needed.

The official PyTorch DQN tutorial provides the complete CartPole example and training curves. Inspect select_action , the replay buffer, gather for chosen-action values, and the torch.no_grad() target inside optimize_model rather than duplicating the whole training program here.

Policy gradients, advantages, and actor–critic

Value-based methods infer an action from estimated returns. Policy-gradient methods directly adjust a differentiable policy πθ\pi_\theta to increase expected return. For discrete actions, a network can produce logits for a categorical distribution; for continuous actions, it can parameterize a suitable probability density.

For complete finite episodes, define J(θ)=𝔼τ∼πθ[G0]J(\theta)=\mathbb E_{\tau\sim\pi_\theta}[G_0] . A reward-to-go policy gradient is

∇θJ=𝔼[∑t=0T−1γtGt∇θlogπθ(At∣St)].\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tG_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\right].

The explicit γt\gamma^t matches the discounted start-state objective above. Undiscounted finite-horizon formulations set γ=1\gamma=1 ; alternative sampling or weighting conventions must not be silently interchanged.

REINFORCE estimates this gradient using sampled returns. It does not need to differentiate through the environment: it differentiates the probability of the sampled action sequence. An action-independent baseline bt(St)b_t(S_t) can be subtracted without changing the expected score-function gradient. A good value baseline often reduces variance, but an arbitrary baseline need not do so.

An actor–critic learns a policy (the actor) together with a value estimator (the critic). The advantage

Aπ(s,a)=Qπ(s,a)−Vπ(s)A^\pi(s,a)=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)

measures an action relative to the policy’s usual performance in that state. A one-step TD residual supplies an advantage estimate: with an exact critic its conditional expectation is the advantage; an approximate critic introduces error. In the actor’s surrogate loss, treat the return/advantage weights as fixed—typically with .detach() —and train the critic with its own objective. Policy-gradient derivation and PyTorch example ↗

Step by step Why the policy gradient uses log probabilities and returns

Setting. Consider complete episodes padded to a finite horizon TT with zero rewards after termination. The initial-state law and transition/reward kernel do not depend on policy parameters θ\theta . Assume a differentiable policy with parameter-independent support and conditions permitting differentiation under the expectation. Expectations below are under the current policy. The derivation keeps the discount factors explicit.

1 · Write a trajectory’s probability

A trajectory is τ=(s0,a0,r1,s1,…,aT−1,rT,sT)\tau=(s_0,a_0,r_1,s_1,\ldots,a_{T-1},r_T,s_T) . Multiplying the initial-state probability and each conditional choice/transition gives

pθ(τ)=ρ0(s0)∏t=0T−1πθ(at∣st)p(st+1,rt+1∣st,at).p_\theta(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi_\theta(a_t\mid s_t)p(s_{t+1},r_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t).

Only the policy factors involve θ\theta . Continuous quantities use densities rather than point probabilities.

2 · Differentiate expected return

Write J(θ)=∑τpθ(τ)G0(τ)J(\theta)=\sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau) . For a fixed trajectory, G0(τ)G_0(\tau) is fixed. From the derivative of a logarithm,

∇θlogpθ(τ)=∇θpθ(τ)pθ(τ).\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\frac{\nabla_\theta p_\theta(\tau)}{p_\theta(\tau)}.

Rearrange and substitute into the derivative of JJ :

∇θJ=∑τpθ(τ)G0(τ)∇θlogpθ(τ)=𝔼[G0∇θlogpθ(τ)].\nabla_\theta J=\sum_\tau p_\theta(\tau)G_0(\tau)\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau) =\mathbb E[G_0\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)].

This is the score-function identity. For continuous trajectories, make the same argument with integrals.

3 · Differentiate the log of the product

Taking the log converts the policy product into a sum. Initial-state and environment terms differentiate to zero:

∇θlogpθ(τ)=∑t=0T−1∇θlogπθ(At∣St).\nabla_\theta\log p_\theta(\tau)=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t).

Therefore

∇θJ=𝔼[∑t=0T−1G0∇θlogπθ(At∣St)].\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}G_0\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\right].

No derivative through the environment appears. We differentiate how likely the sampled trajectory is, not how a black-box environment changes when an action changes.

4 · Prove why past rewards contribute zero

Let ℋt\mathcal H_t be all history before choosing AtA_t . Conditional on it, past rewards are fixed. The expected policy score is

𝔼[∇θlogπθ(At∣St)∣ℋt]=∑aπθ(a∣St)∇θπθ(a∣St)πθ(a∣St)\mathbb E[\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\mid\mathcal H_t] =\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid S_t)\frac{\nabla_\theta\pi_\theta(a\mid S_t)}{\pi_\theta(a\mid S_t)}
=∇θ∑aπθ(a∣St)=∇θ1=0.=\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid S_t)=\nabla_\theta1=0.

Multiplying by any fixed past reward and then averaging still gives zero. Thus only rewards resulting from decision tt or later need to weight its score.

5 · Keep the remaining discount factors

Removing past rewards leaves ∑k=tT−1γkRk+1\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^k R_{k+1} . Factor out γt\gamma^t :

∑k=tT−1γkRk+1=γt∑k=tT−1γk−tRk+1=γtGt.\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^kR_{k+1} =\gamma^t\sum_{k=t}^{T-1}\gamma^{k-t}R_{k+1}=\gamma^tG_t.

Substitute this into the score expression:

∇θJ=𝔼[∑t=0T−1γtGt∇θlogπθ(At∣St)].\nabla_\theta J=\mathbb E\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\gamma^tG_t\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)\right].

This is the finite-horizon discounted, start-state objective’s gradient. Dropping γt\gamma^t while keeping discounted GtG_t generally gives a different estimator unless the weighting/sampling convention accounts for it.

6 · Show that an action-independent baseline has zero expectation

For a fixed state/time and a baseline bt(s)b_t(s) treated as fixed,

∑aπθ(a∣s)bt(s)∇θlogπθ(a∣s)=bt(s)∇θ∑aπθ(a∣s)=0.\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)b_t(s)\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(a\mid s) =b_t(s)\nabla_\theta\sum_a\pi_\theta(a\mid s)=0.

Consequently Gt−bt(St)G_t-b_t(S_t) can replace GtG_t without changing the expected score gradient. The baseline must not depend on the current sampled action; when fitted from the same samples, the simple conditional proof needs extra care. A critic computed from earlier data and held fixed during the actor update is the intended construction here.

7 · Connect return weights to advantage

Conditioning on St=s,At=aS_t=s,A_t=a gives 𝔼[Gt∣s,a]=Qπ(s,a)\mathbb E[G_t\mid s,a]=Q^\pi(s,a) . With the exact value baseline,

𝔼[Gt−Vπ(St)∣s,a]=Qπ(s,a)−Vπ(s)=Aπ(s,a).\mathbb E[G_t-V^\pi(S_t)\mid s,a]=Q^\pi(s,a)-V^\pi(s)=A^\pi(s,a).

The same is true for the conditional expectation of a one-step TD residual when its critic is exact. Approximate bootstrapped critics can add bias; the equality does not make every practical advantage estimator exact.

8 · Convert ascent into a loss for automatic differentiation

For NN freshly sampled complete episodes, a local surrogate loss is

L̂actor(θ)=−1N∑i=1N∑tγtsg(Gi,t−bt(Si,t))logπθ(Ai,t∣Si,t).\widehat L_{\mathrm{actor}}(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_t \gamma^t\,\operatorname{sg}\!\bigl(G_{i,t}-b_t(S_{i,t})\bigr) \log\pi_\theta(A_{i,t}\mid S_{i,t}).

Here sg\operatorname{sg} means stop-gradient. At the parameters that collected the data, the negative gradient is the sampled ascent estimator. The division is by episodes for the stated objective; averaging all transitions can change weighting, especially with variable episode lengths. The scalar surrogate’s absolute value is not a direct measure of policy quality, and stale off-policy data require a different estimator or correction.

Where PPO fits

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO-Clip) is an actor–critic policy-gradient method that compares the new policy with the policy used to collect the current batch. Its clipped surrogate limits the incentive to make an already-favorable probability change much larger. It is not a hard bound on every probability ratio, nor a guarantee that true return increases after an update.

Step by step PPO clipping: follow the positive and negative advantage cases

Setting. A batch was collected with πold\pi_{\mathrm{old}} . Freeze its log probabilities and advantage estimates Ât\hat A_t while optimizing the actor. Let 0<ϵ<10<\epsilon<1 . The following explains PPO-Clip’s chosen surrogate; it is not a derivation of a guaranteed improvement in the true return.

1 · Compare the new and old probability of the sampled action

rt(θ)=πθ(At∣St)πold(At∣St)=exp(logπθ(At∣St)−logπold(At∣St)).r_t(\theta)=\frac{\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)}{\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(A_t\mid S_t)} =\exp\!\left(\log\pi_\theta(A_t\mid S_t)-\log\pi_{\mathrm{old}}(A_t\mid S_t)\right).

A ratio above one means that the new policy assigns greater probability to that action. This rtr_t is a probability ratio , not the reward Rt+1R_{t+1} .

2 · Write the per-sample clipped objective

ℓtclip=min(rtÂt,clip(rt,1−ϵ,1+ϵ)Ât).\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}=\min\!\left(r_t\hat A_t,\ \operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\hat A_t\right).

The actor maximizes the batch average; a minimization optimizer uses its negative. Value and entropy terms are separate additions in a complete implementation.

3 · Handle the advantage signs separately

If Ât>0\hat A_t>0 , increasing the ratio improves the unclipped term. Comparing the two terms gives

ℓtclip=Âtmin(rt,1+ϵ).\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}=\hat A_t\min(r_t,1+\epsilon).

If Ât<0\hat A_t<0 , multiplication reverses the ordering, so

ℓtclip=Âtmax(rt,1−ϵ).\ell_t^{\mathrm{clip}}=\hat A_t\max(r_t,1-\epsilon).

For zero advantage both are zero. Only the incentive for an excessive favorable change is flattened: increased probability for a positive-advantage action, or decreased probability for a negative-advantage action.

4 · Substitute numbers and interpret the limit

With ϵ=0.2\epsilon=0.2 , positive advantage Â=2\hat A=2 , and ratio r=1.4r=1.4 , the two terms are 2.82.8 and 2.42.4 , so the contribution is 2.42.4 . With Â=−2\hat A=-2 and r=0.6r=0.6 , the two terms are −1.2-1.2 and −1.6-1.6 , so it is −1.6-1.6 .

The ratio can still leave the interval because parameters are shared across observations and other loss terms also update them. Clipping therefore does not enforce a global trust-region constraint. Practical checks before trusting a result

Termination is not truncation. A true terminal state has no continuation value. An artificial rollout/time cutoff in an otherwise continuing task normally still needs bootstrapping from the final observation. End the collection episode on terminated or truncated , but suppress the bootstrap on terminated . A genuine finite task horizon is part of the state/task definition. Never bootstrap from the next episode’s reset observation. Gymnasium’s worked explanation ↗

Evaluate the behavior, not only the surrogate loss. Check held-out rollouts, multiple random seeds, episode returns, failure rates, and environment interactions used. A low TD or actor loss does not by itself mean a useful policy. Keep evaluation settings explicit, including whether actions are sampled or chosen deterministically. A reward is a proxy for the task: maximizing it can exploit a simulator shortcut or an unintended incentive. Evaluation and debugging guidance ↗

This is a foundations supplement, not a complete treatment of continuous-control algorithms, offline RL, model-based planning, or reinforcement learning from human feedback. The aim is to connect familiar networks, gradients, and losses to the extra issue RL introduces: the learner’s decisions influence its future training experience .

Check your understanding Why is predicting the next reward not enough?

An action changes the next state and therefore later opportunities. Immediate-reward prediction omits those later consequences; a return and its value function account for them under the chosen policy and discount convention.
