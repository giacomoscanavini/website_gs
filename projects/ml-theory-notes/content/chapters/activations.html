Two jobs, not one

An activation shapes the function evaluated in the forward pass. Its derivative also scales the learning signal in the backward pass:

a=ϕ(z),∂L∂z=∂L∂aϕ′(z).a=\phi(z),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\phi'(z).

That is why activation choice affects both representational capacity and trainability. Two functions can have a similar output range yet behave differently under optimization because their derivatives differ.

ReLU: a piecewise-linear gate

ReLU(z)=max(0,z),ReLU′(z)={1z>00z<0.\operatorname{ReLU}(z)=\max(0,z),\qquad \operatorname{ReLU}'(z)=\begin{cases}1&z>0\\0&z<0.\end{cases}

For positive inputs, the activation transmits the incoming derivative without attenuation. For negative inputs, it blocks that path for the observation. At exactly zero, the mathematical derivative is not defined; the source notes that software typically uses zero as a convention.

A dead ReLU remains in the inactive region across relevant observations and therefore receives no useful gradient through that activation. Leaky ReLU replaces the negative branch with αz\alpha z , giving a small nonzero slope instead of zero. This does not guarantee a useful representation, but it prevents a strictly zero derivative on that branch.

Sigmoid: probability interpretation and saturation

σ(z)=11+e−z,σ′(z)=σ(z)(1−σ(z)).\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-z}},\qquad \sigma'(z)=\sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)).

Its output lies between zero and one. At z=0z=0 , the output is 0.50.5 and its derivative is 0.250.25 , the maximum. At large positive or negative logits it saturates: changing the logit produces only a small probability change.

This makes sigmoid natural for modeling a binary probability but problematic when repeated as the hidden activation in a deep network. Even before accounting for weights, multiplying many derivatives no larger than 0.250.25 can produce a tiny sensitivity.

The important nuance is the loss–activation pair . With a binary cross-entropy loss, differentiating with respect to the logit simplifies to σ(z)−y\sigma(z)-y . Do not reason about output-layer training by looking at the sigmoid derivative alone while ignoring the derivative of the loss. The loss chapter explains the pairing.

Step by step Derive sigmoid’s derivative and maximum slope

1 · Differentiate the reciprocal

Write σ(z)=(1+e−z)−1\sigma(z)=(1+e^{-z})^{-1} . For u(z)−1u(z)^{-1} , the derivative is −u(z)−2u′(z)-u(z)^{-2}u'(z) . Here u′(z)=−e−zu'(z)=-e^{-z} , so

σ′(z)=−(1+e−z)−2(−e−z)=e−z(1+e−z)2.\sigma'(z)=-(1+e^{-z})^{-2}(-e^{-z})=\frac{e^{-z}}{(1+e^{-z})^2}.

1−σ(z)=1−11+e−z=e−z1+e−z.1-\sigma(z)=1-\frac1{1+e^{-z}}=\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}.

Multiply the two factors:

σ(z)[1−σ(z)]=11+e−ze−z1+e−z=σ′(z).\sigma(z)[1-\sigma(z)]=\frac1{1+e^{-z}}\frac{e^{-z}}{1+e^{-z}}=\sigma'(z).

3 · Find the largest possible slope

Let p=σ(z)p=\sigma(z) , so 0<p<10<p<1 . Completing the square gives

p(1−p)=p−p2=14−(p−12)2≤14.p(1-p)=p-p^2=\frac14-\left(p-\frac12\right)^2\le\frac14.

Equality requires p=1/2p=1/2 , attained at z=0z=0 . This explains both the peak derivative 1/41/4 and the flat tails as p→0p\to0 or p→1p\to1 .

4 · Carry an upstream gradient through it

If g=∂L/∂pg=\partial L/\partial p , then ∂L/∂z=gp(1−p)\partial L/\partial z=g\,p(1-p) . The maximum slope bounds the activation contribution . It does not bound the entire network gradient because weights and the loss derivative also contribute. In the binary cross-entropy pairing, the latter cancels this factor; see the logit derivation in the loss chapter.

Tanh: centered, but still saturating

tanh(z)=ez−e−zez+e−z,tanh′(z)=1−tanh2(z).\tanh(z)=\frac{e^z-e^{-z}}{e^z+e^{-z}},\qquad \tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2(z).

Tanh maps to (−1,1)(-1,1) and is centered at zero. It is useful when a signed bounded state is desired, as in recurrent candidates. But large-magnitude inputs still move it into flat regions. Zero centering and resistance to saturation are different properties.

1 · Name the numerator and denominator

Let u=ez−e−zu=e^z-e^{-z} and v=ez+e−zv=e^z+e^{-z} . Then u′=ez+e−z=vu'=e^z+e^{-z}=v and v′=ez−e−z=uv'=e^z-e^{-z}=u .

ddzuv=u′v−uv′v2=v2−u2v2=1−(uv)2.\frac{d}{dz}\frac uv=\frac{u'v-uv'}{v^2}=\frac{v^2-u^2}{v^2}=1-\left(\frac uv\right)^2.

Since u/v=tanhzu/v=\tanh z ,

tanh′(z)=1−tanh2z.\tanh'(z)=1-\tanh^2z.

v2−u2=(ez+e−z)2−(ez−e−z)2=4eze−z=4.v^2-u^2=(e^z+e^{-z})^2-(e^z-e^{-z})^2=4e^ze^{-z}=4.

Thus the derivative is positive for finite zz , equals one at zero, and approaches zero in either tail.

Differentiating this second expression gives 4σ(2z)[1−σ(2z)]4\sigma(2z)[1-\sigma(2z)] , which equals 1−tanh2z1-\tanh^2z . It is an independent algebraic check of the same result.

GELU: smooth modulation

The source defines

GELU(z)=zΦ(z),\operatorname{GELU}(z)=z\Phi(z),

where Φ\Phi is the standard normal cumulative distribution function, and gives the common approximation

GELU(z)≈z2[1+tanh(2π(z+0.044715z3))].\operatorname{GELU}(z)\approx \frac z2\left[1+\tanh\left(\sqrt{\frac2\pi}(z+0.044715z^3)\right)\right].

Rather than applying ReLU’s abrupt cutoff, GELU changes the contribution smoothly. It can produce small negative outputs. The function’s name and probabilistic expression do not mean that the layer returns class probabilities.

Step by step Derive the exact and approximate GELU gradients

Notation. Write Φ\Phi for the standard normal cumulative distribution and φ(z)=(2π)−1/2e−z2/2\varphi(z)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/2} for its density. The density symbol here is not the generic activation ϕ\phi .

By definition, Φ(z)=∫−∞zφ(t)dt\Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^z\varphi(t)\,dt . The fundamental theorem of calculus gives Φ′(z)=φ(z)\Phi'(z)=\varphi(z) . Apply the product rule to zΦ(z)z\Phi(z) :

GELU′(z)=1⋅Φ(z)+zΦ′(z)=Φ(z)+zφ(z).\operatorname{GELU}'(z)=1\cdot\Phi(z)+z\Phi'(z)=\Phi(z)+z\varphi(z).

At z=0z=0 the derivative is 1/21/2 . A smooth derivative is not necessarily positive everywhere; GELU has a mildly nonmonotonic negative-input region.

Set c=2/πc=\sqrt{2/\pi} , a=0.044715a=0.044715 , and u(z)=c(z+az3)u(z)=c(z+az^3) . The approximation is g(z)=z[1+tanhu(z)]/2g(z)=z[1+\tanh u(z)]/2 . First,

u′(z)=c(1+3az2).u'(z)=c(1+3az^2).

Then the product rule and chain rule give

g′(z)=12[1+tanhu(z)]+z2[1−tanh2u(z)]c(1+3az2).g'(z)=\frac12[1+\tanh u(z)]+\frac z2[1-\tanh^2u(z)]c(1+3az^2).

Do not combine the exact forward formula with this approximate backward formula. They differentiate different functions.

3 · Contrast the piecewise ReLU calculation

For z>0z>0 , max(0,z)=z\max(0,z)=z and its derivative is one. For z<0z<0 it is the constant zero and its derivative is zero. At zero the left and right slopes disagree, so an ordinary derivative does not exist. Choosing zero in software is a convention, not a proof of differentiability. Leaky ReLU changes the negative-branch slope to its specified α\alpha .

Hidden activations versus output activations

Hidden activations help construct a useful nonlinear representation. Output activations must match the target: a real-valued regression target may require no restriction, a binary probability uses sigmoid, and mutually exclusive class probabilities use softmax.

An output ReLU would make negative predictions impossible, which is wrong for an unrestricted signed target. Conversely, an unrestricted scalar is not automatically a probability. In PyTorch, numerical stability often means passing raw logits into a fused loss , then applying sigmoid or softmax only when probabilities are needed. The conceptual probability mapping is not a mandate to insert that mapping before every loss call.

Check your understanding Does using ReLU solve vanishing gradients?

No. On an active unit its local derivative is one, so that activation does not shrink the gradient. Inactive units have zero derivative, and weight matrices elsewhere can still contract or amplify gradients. ReLU removes one source of attenuation, not every source.
