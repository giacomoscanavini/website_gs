One global step scale may not suit every coordinate

Some parameters receive large or frequent gradients; others receive small or infrequent ones. A single learning rate can be too aggressive for the first group and too weak for the second. The guide represents adaptation as

θt+1=θt−ηDt−1gt,\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta D_t^{-1}g_t,

where DtD_t is usually a positive diagonal matrix constructed from past squared gradients. This is a cheap diagonal preconditioner : it rescales each coordinate without modeling every cross-coordinate interaction.

It does not measure the Hessian directly. Gradient magnitude history and curvature are related only indirectly, so adaptive scaling is not synonymous with Newton’s method.

AdaGrad remembers every squared gradient

Starting with G0=0G_0=0 ,

Gt=Gt−1+gt⊙gt,θt+1=θt−ηgtGt+ϵ.G_t=G_{t-1}+g_t\odot g_t,\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\frac{g_t}{\sqrt{G_t}+\epsilon}.

Operations are elementwise. For coordinate jj , the denominator contains ∑s≤tgs,j2\sum_{s\le t}g_{s,j}^2 . Frequently active coordinates accumulate more history and receive smaller effective steps. Infrequently active coordinates retain a larger scale for longer.

The small ϵ\epsilon protects the division and affects behavior when the accumulator is tiny. The expression here follows the guide’s square-root-then-add convention; actual numerical comparisons should respect a library’s epsilon placement and other options.

AdaGrad’s worked example

The source uses η=1\eta=1 , negligible ϵ\epsilon , G0=0G_0=0 , and g1=(3,1)⊤g_1=(3,1)^\top . Then G1=(9,1)⊤G_1=(9,1)^\top , and the normalized gradient is (1,1)⊤(1,1)^\top .

For g2=(3,0)⊤g_2=(3,0)^\top , the accumulator becomes (18,1)⊤(18,1)^\top . Coordinate one’s normalized gradient is 3/18≈0.7073/\sqrt{18}\approx0.707 ; coordinate two does not move because its current gradient is zero. A larger available scale does not create an update in the absence of a gradient.

The weakness follows from the same mechanism: the accumulator never forgets. Effective rates can become too small after a long history even when more learning is needed.

Step by step Expand AdaGrad’s history and effective step size

Setting. The AdaGrad recurrence is an algorithmic design choice. The derivation here exposes its consequences; it is not claimed to follow uniquely from minimizing every loss.

1 · Expand the accumulator

Starting from G0,j=0G_{0,j}=0 ,

G1,j=g1,j2,G2,j=g1,j2+g2,j2,Gt,j=∑s=1tgs,j2.G_{1,j}=g_{1,j}^2,\quad G_{2,j}=g_{1,j}^2+g_{2,j}^2,\quad G_{t,j}=\sum_{s=1}^t g_{s,j}^2.

Every term is nonnegative, so Gt+1,j≥Gt,jG_{t+1,j}\ge G_{t,j} .

2 · Isolate the coordinate multiplier

Δθt,j=−ηGt,j+ϵgt,j.\Delta\theta_{t,j}=-\frac{\eta}{\sqrt{G_{t,j}}+\epsilon}g_{t,j}.

For fixed positive η\eta and ϵ\epsilon , the multiplier cannot increase as history accumulates. That is a statement about the multiplier; the actual displacement also depends on the current gradient.

3 · Compute both steps from the source

With g1=(3,1)g_1=(3,1) , G1=(9,1)G_1=(9,1) and negligible ϵ\epsilon , the normalized gradient is (3/3,1/1)=(1,1)(3/3,1/1)=(1,1) . With g2=(3,0)g_2=(3,0) ,

G2=(9+9,1+0)=(18,1),g2G2=(318,0)=(12,0).G_2=(9+9,1+0)=(18,1),\qquad \frac{g_2}{\sqrt{G_2}}=\left(\frac3{\sqrt{18}},0\right)=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2},0\right).

4 · Derive the repeated-gradient decay

If one coordinate receives the same nonzero gradient gg for tt steps, Gt=tg2G_t=tg^2 and Gt=t|g|\sqrt{G_t}=\sqrt t\,|g| . Ignoring ϵ\epsilon ,

Δθt=−ηtsign(g).\Delta\theta_t=-\frac\eta{\sqrt t}\operatorname{sign}(g).

If it has been active only kk times, the corresponding nonzero-step magnitude is η/k\eta/\sqrt k . Sparse activity therefore retains a larger available scale, but zero current gradient still gives zero movement.

5 · Treat zero history explicitly

With Gt=0G_t=0 and gt=0g_t=0 , the implementation’s positive ϵ\epsilon prevents 0/00/0 . Sending ϵ\epsilon to zero is legitimate only in numerical examples whose denominators remain positive.

RMSProp remembers the recent scale

RMSProp replaces the cumulative sum with an exponentially weighted average:

vt=ρvt−1+(1−ρ)(gt⊙gt),θt+1=θt−ηgtvt+ϵ.v_t=\rho v_{t-1}+(1-\rho)(g_t\odot g_t),\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta\frac{g_t}{\sqrt{v_t}+\epsilon}.

If recent gradients become smaller, old large values decay and the denominator can shrink again. This is the core reason RMSProp can respond to changing gradient scales where AdaGrad’s permanent history cannot.

With ρ=0.9\rho=0.9 , η=0.1\eta=0.1 , v0=0v_0=0 , and g1=2g_1=2 , the source computes v1=0.4v_1=0.4 and an update magnitude near 0.3160.316 . If g2=1g_2=1 , then v2=0.46v_2=0.46 and the magnitude is near 0.1470.147 .

These numbers show why the base learning rate is not a literal update magnitude. The ratio of gradient to its scale estimate modifies the result.

Step by step Expand RMSProp’s decaying second moment

Setting. vt=ρvt−1+(1−ρ)gt2v_t=\rho v_{t-1}+(1-\rho)g_t^2 , 0≤ρ<10\le\rho<1 , applied coordinatewise. The usual denominator shown here is vt+ϵ\sqrt{v_t}+\epsilon .

1 · Substitute the previous recurrence

vt=ρ[ρvt−2+(1−ρ)gt−12]+(1−ρ)gt2.v_t=\rho[\rho v_{t-2}+(1-\rho)g_{t-1}^2]+(1-\rho)g_t^2.

Continuing to v0v_0 gives

vt=ρtv0+(1−ρ)∑j=1tρt−jgj2.v_t=\rho^t v_0+(1-\rho)\sum_{j=1}^t\rho^{t-j}g_j^2.

The weights of the observed squared gradients sum to 1−ρt1-\rho^t .

2 · Interpret the initialization effect

If v0=0v_0=0 and 𝔼[gj2]=s\mathbb E[g_j^2]=s is constant over time, linearity of expectation gives

𝔼[vt]=(1−ρ)s∑k=0t−1ρk=(1−ρt)s.\mathbb E[v_t]=(1-\rho)s\sum_{k=0}^{t-1}\rho^k=(1-\rho^t)s.

This estimate is initially below ss . Standard uncentered RMSProp does not automatically apply Adam’s 1−ρt1-\rho^t correction; that is an algorithmic difference.

4 · See how old large gradients disappear

If future gradients are zero, the recurrence becomes vt+k=ρkvtv_{t+k}=\rho^k v_t . Therefore the scale estimate decays instead of remaining permanently large as in AdaGrad. The exact e-fold timescale is −1/logρ-1/\log\rho , with the same near-one approximation as momentum.

5 · Do not call this a variance without centering

vtv_t tracks a raw second moment. A variance is 𝔼[g2]−𝔼[g]2\mathbb E[g^2]-\mathbb E[g]^2 . Centered RMSProp variants estimate both terms; the plain recurrence above does not. Also, 𝔼[1/vt]\mathbb E[1/\sqrt{v_t}] is generally not 1/𝔼[vt]1/\sqrt{\mathbb E[v_t]} , so moment expectations alone do not give the expected parameter step.

What this adaptation does not solve

Diagonal scaling treats coordinates independently. It cannot generally undo a rotated, highly elongated valley as a full curvature model could. It also cannot repair bad labels, leakage, a detached graph, or inappropriate input transformations. Feature scaling and initialization remain relevant even when the optimizer is adaptive.

The comparison to remember is permanent history versus recent history , not “old optimizer versus new optimizer.” Whether either method is useful depends on the gradient process and the task. Adam adds a smoothed numerator to this adaptive-denominator idea.

Check your understanding Why can AdaGrad become ineffective late in training?

Its squared-gradient accumulator never decreases. With continued nonzero gradients, the denominator grows and the effective coordinate learning rate can become very small. RMSProp replaces that permanent history with a decaying average that can respond to a new scale.
