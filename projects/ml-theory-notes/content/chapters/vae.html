Variational autoencoders and the ELBO

A variational autoencoder learns a latent-variable generative model. Its encoder approximates the posterior, and its objective balances reconstruction likelihood with divergence from a latent prior.

Replace a point code with a conditional distribution

A deterministic autoencoder assigns a code to an input. A VAE encoder parameterizes an approximate posterior, commonly

qϕ(z∣x)=𝒩(μϕ(x),diag(σϕ2(x))).q_\phi(z\mid x)=\mathcal N\left(\mu_\phi(x),\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_\phi^2(x))\right).

The latent prior is often p(z)=𝒩(0,I)p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I) , and a decoder specifies a likelihood pθ(x∣z)p_\theta(x\mid z) . The goal is not merely to reconstruct one point through a noisy layer; it is to learn a latent-variable generative model with a tractable approximation to inference.

The evidence lower bound

The evidence lower bound, or ELBO, is

ℒELBO(x)=𝔼qϕ(z∣x)[logpθ(x∣z)]−KL(qϕ(z∣x)‖p(z)).\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x)=\mathbb E_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)] -\operatorname{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p(z)).

Training minimizes the negative ELBO, equivalently maximizing this lower bound. The expectation rewards likelihood of the observed input under latent samples. The KL term pulls the approximate posterior toward the chosen prior.

A “reconstruction loss” is therefore a negative log-likelihood term under a particular observation model, not an arbitrary interchangeable distance. The decoder’s output distribution determines what loss is consistent with it.

Why the ELBO is a lower bound

Introduce qϕ(z∣x)q_\phi(z\mid x) and rewrite

logpθ(x)=ℒELBO(x)+KL(qϕ(z∣x)‖pθ(z∣x)).\log p_\theta(x)=\mathcal L_{\mathrm{ELBO}}(x)+ \operatorname{KL}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\Vert p_\theta(z\mid x)).

The last term is nonnegative, so the ELBO cannot exceed the log evidence. Equality holds when the approximate posterior matches the model posterior, subject to the usual distribution/support conditions.

Expanding the ELBO expectation as 𝔼q[logpθ(x,z)−logqϕ(z∣x)]\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)] and using pθ(x,z)=pθ(x∣z)p(z)p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z) gives the reconstruction-likelihood and prior-divergence terms above. This separates the tractable training bound from the generally difficult exact posterior.

Step by step Derive the evidence bound and identify its exact gap

Setting. Fix an observed xx with pθ(x)>0p_\theta(x)>0 . Let qϕ(z∣x)q_\phi(z\mid x) be a normalized density, with support compatible with the following ratios and finite expectations. All logarithms are natural.

1 · Introduce the latent variable and an auxiliary density

Marginalization gives pθ(x)=∫pθ(x,z)dzp_\theta(x)=\int p_\theta(x,z)\,dz . If qq covers the relevant joint support, multiply and divide the integrand by qq :

pθ(x)=∫qϕ(z∣x)pθ(x,z)qϕ(z∣x)dz=𝔼q[pθ(x,z)qϕ(z∣x)].p_\theta(x)=\int q_\phi(z\mid x)\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\,dz =\mathbb E_q\left[\frac{p_\theta(x,z)}{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\right].

2 · Apply concavity of the logarithm

For positive RR , Jensen’s inequality says log𝔼[R]≥𝔼[logR]\log\mathbb E[R]\ge\mathbb E[\log R] . Therefore

logpθ(x)≥𝔼q[logpθ(x,z)−logqϕ(z∣x)]≡ℰ(θ,ϕ;x).\log p_\theta(x)\ge\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x,z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)]\equiv\mathcal E(\theta,\phi;x).

This quantity ℰ\mathcal E is the evidence lower bound, or ELBO.

3 · Factor the joint model

Use pθ(x,z)=pθ(x∣z)p(z)p_\theta(x,z)=p_\theta(x\mid z)p(z) . Logarithms split the product:

ℰ=𝔼q[logpθ(x∣z)]+𝔼q[logp(z)−logqϕ(z∣x)].\mathcal E=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)] +\mathbb E_q[\log p(z)-\log q_\phi(z\mid x)].

The second term is negative KL, yielding

ℰ=𝔼q[logpθ(x∣z)]−DKL(qϕ(z∣x)∥p(z)).\mathcal E=\mathbb E_q[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]- D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q_\phi(z\mid x)\|p(z)).

4 · Derive the gap directly using Bayes’ rule

Since pθ(z∣x)=pθ(x,z)/pθ(x)p_\theta(z\mid x)=p_\theta(x,z)/p_\theta(x) ,

DKL(q∥pθ(z∣x))=𝔼q[logq−logpθ(x,z)+logpθ(x)].D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_\theta(z\mid x)) =\mathbb E_q[\log q-\log p_\theta(x,z)+\log p_\theta(x)].

The evidence is constant with respect to zz , so this becomes

DKL(q∥pθ(z∣x))=logpθ(x)−ℰ.D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p_\theta(z\mid x))=\log p_\theta(x)-\mathcal E.

Rearranging gives an exact identity, not merely an inequality. The KL-to- posterior is the gap; the KL-to- prior is the term appearing in the decomposed training bound. They are different objects.

5 · Justify nonnegativity

For normalized compatible densities q,pq,p , concavity gives

𝔼qlogpq≤log𝔼qpq≤log1=0.\mathbb E_q\log\frac pq\le\log\mathbb E_q\frac pq\le\log1=0.

Negating yields DKL(q∥p)≥0D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)\ge0 . Equality requires matching distributions almost everywhere. Thus the bound is tight exactly when the variational posterior matches the true posterior under the stated conditions.

6 · Convert the bound to a minimized objective

Training typically minimizes −ℰ=𝔼q[−logpθ(x∣z)]+DKL(q∥p(z))-\mathcal E=\mathbb E_q[-\log p_\theta(x\mid z)]+D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p(z)) . A changed coefficient on KL or an independently rescaled reconstruction loss changes this objective; it is not automatically the same model’s exact negative ELBO.

Reparameterization separates randomness from parameters

Directly sampling from a parameter-dependent distribution obscures an ordinary differentiable path. Reparameterization writes

ϵ∼𝒩(0,I),z=μϕ(x)+σϕ(x)⊙ϵ.\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I),\qquad z=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\epsilon.

The random draw now has a fixed parameter-independent distribution. For a sampled ϵ\epsilon , the transformation from μ,σ\mu,\sigma to zz is differentiable. A Monte Carlo estimate can then pass gradients through the decoder and into the encoder parameters.

This does not eliminate sampling variation. It gives a usable gradient path for the selected distributional family.

Step by step Differentiate through Gaussian reparameterization

Setting. Draw ε∼𝒩(0,I)\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I) independently of model parameters. Define z=μϕ(x)+σϕ(x)⊙εz=\mu_\phi(x)+\sigma_\phi(x)\odot\varepsilon . Assume enough regularity to exchange differentiation and expectation.

1 · Verify the sample distribution

Each coordinate has mean μj+σj𝔼[εj]=μj\mu_j+\sigma_j\mathbb E[\varepsilon_j]=\mu_j and variance σj2Var(εj)=σj2\sigma_j^2\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_j)=\sigma_j^2 . Independent noise coordinates yield diagonal covariance, so this construction samples the desired Gaussian.

2 · Rewrite the expectation over fixed base noise

For a differentiable function FF , the equal distributions imply

𝔼z∼qϕ[F(z)]=𝔼ε[F(μϕ+σϕ⊙ε)].\mathbb E_{z\sim q_\phi}[F(z)]=\mathbb E_\varepsilon[F(\mu_\phi+\sigma_\phi\odot\varepsilon)].

The distribution on the right does not move when ϕ\phi changes; only the transformation does.

3 · Apply the chain rule to a sampled computation

Let gj=∂F/∂zjg_j=\partial F/\partial z_j . Holding the sampled ε\varepsilon fixed,

∂F∂μj=gj,∂F∂σj=gjεj.\frac{\partial F}{\partial\mu_j}=g_j,\qquad \frac{\partial F}{\partial\sigma_j}=g_j\varepsilon_j.

If the network emits ℓj=logσj2\ell_j=\log\sigma_j^2 , then σj=eℓj/2\sigma_j=e^{\ell_j/2} and

∂σj∂ℓj=12eℓj/2=12σj,\frac{\partial\sigma_j}{\partial\ell_j}=\tfrac12e^{\ell_j/2}=\tfrac12\sigma_j,

so ∂F/∂ℓj=gjεjσj/2\partial F/\partial\ell_j=g_j\varepsilon_j\sigma_j/2 .

4 · Continue to encoder parameters

∇ϕ𝔼[F(z)]=𝔼ε[∑jgj(∇ϕμj+εj∇ϕσj)].\nabla_\phi\mathbb E[F(z)] =\mathbb E_\varepsilon\left[\sum_j g_j\left(\nabla_\phi\mu_j+\varepsilon_j\nabla_\phi\sigma_j\right)\right].

A sample average of the bracketed term estimates this gradient. If FF also depends explicitly on ϕ\phi , its direct derivative must be added; the formula above assumes its parameter dependence is through zz .

5 · Interpret the randomness correctly

We do not differentiate the random-number generator. We differentiate a deterministic transformation of already sampled noise, then average over such computations. This is a pathwise gradient estimator; it does not by itself establish a valid pathwise estimator for discrete categorical sampling.

KL divergence for a diagonal Gaussian

For a diagonal Gaussian posterior and standard normal prior, the KL term is

KL(q‖p)=12∑j(μj2+σj2−1−logσj2).\operatorname{KL}(q\Vert p)=\frac12\sum_j\left(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\right).

If μ=0\mu=0 and every σj2=1\sigma_j^2=1, the divergence is zero. Moving a mean away from zero or changing the variances incurs a penalty.

The code predicts a log variance and computes the standard deviation as exp(12logσ2)\exp(\tfrac12\log\sigma^2) . Confusing variance with standard deviation changes the sampled distribution. The example also shows how the reconstruction and KL reductions are combined; their relative scale must remain intentional.

Step by step Derive every term of the diagonal-Gaussian KL

Setting. Let q(z∣x)=𝒩(μ,diag(σ12,…,σd2))q(z\mid x)=\mathcal N(\mu,\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_1^2,\ldots,\sigma_d^2)) with every σj>0\sigma_j>0 , and let p(z)=𝒩(0,I)p(z)=\mathcal N(0,I) . The parameters are fixed for the observed xx during this calculation.

1 · Write both log densities

logq(z)=−d2log(2π)−∑jlogσj−12∑j(zj−μj)2σj2,\log q(z)=-\frac d2\log(2\pi)-\sum_j\log\sigma_j -\frac12\sum_j\frac{(z_j-\mu_j)^2}{\sigma_j^2},
logp(z)=−d2log(2π)−12∑jzj2.\log p(z)=-\frac d2\log(2\pi)-\frac12\sum_jz_j^2.

2 · Subtract to cancel the normalization constants

logq(z)p(z)=12∑j[zj2−(zj−μj)2σj2−logσj2].\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)} =\frac12\sum_j\left[z_j^2-\frac{(z_j-\mu_j)^2}{\sigma_j^2}-\log\sigma_j^2\right].

The logarithmic term uses 2logσj=logσj22\log\sigma_j=\log\sigma_j^2 .

3 · Evaluate each expectation under q

By definition, 𝔼q[zj]=μj\mathbb E_q[z_j]=\mu_j and Varq(zj)=σj2\operatorname{Var}_q(z_j)=\sigma_j^2 . Hence

𝔼q[zj2]=σj2+μj2,𝔼q[(zj−μj)2/σj2]=1.\mathbb E_q[z_j^2]=\sigma_j^2+\mu_j^2,\qquad \mathbb E_q[(z_j-\mu_j)^2/\sigma_j^2]=1.

The log variance is constant under this expectation.

4 · Substitute and sum

DKL(q∥p)=12∑j=1d(μj2+σj2−1−logσj2).D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)=\frac12\sum_{j=1}^d \left(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\log\sigma_j^2\right).

For network output ℓj=logσj2\ell_j=\log\sigma_j^2 , substitute σj2=eℓj\sigma_j^2=e^{\ell_j} :

DKL=12∑j(μj2+eℓj−1−ℓj).D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\frac12\sum_j(\mu_j^2+e^{\ell_j}-1-\ell_j).

Each term is nonnegative because u−1−logu≥0u-1-\log u\ge0 for u>0u>0 and μj2≥0\mu_j^2\ge0 .

5 · Differentiate the analytic result

∂DKL∂μj=μj,∂DKL∂ℓj=12(eℓj−1).\frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{KL}}}{\partial\mu_j}=\mu_j,\qquad \frac{\partial D_{\mathrm{KL}}}{\partial\ell_j}=\frac12(e^{\ell_j}-1).

For μ=0\mu=0 and ℓ=0\ell=0 , the posterior equals the prior, so KL and both derivatives vanish. With μ=1,σ=2\mu=1,\sigma=2 , one dimension contributes (1+4−1−log4)/2≈1.30685(1+4-1-\log4)/2\approx1.30685 .

6 · Keep the reduction consistent

The displayed expression sums latent coordinates for one example. A batch objective may average these per-example values, while its reconstruction likelihood normally sums observed dimensions per example. Averaging one term over its dimensions but not the other changes their relative weight.

Read the objective’s trade-off

The encoder is pulled toward codes that support reconstruction and toward posterior distributions compatible with the prior. If the latent variable is ignored, a low KL by itself is not evidence of a useful representation. If reconstruction dominates without an appropriate prior relationship, the generative interpretation can weaken.

Check your understanding What does the VAE KL term do, and is a VAE just an autoencoder with random noise?

The encoder specifies an approximate posterior distribution, and the decoder is a likelihood model. The KL term regularizes that posterior toward a prior as part of a variational lower bound. Sampling alone does not create that objective or probabilistic interpretation.
