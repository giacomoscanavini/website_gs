OLS, Ridge, and spectral shrinkage

Ridge stabilizes least squares by penalizing coefficient magnitude. Its singular-value form shows why weakly identified directions receive the strongest relative shrinkage.

OLS exposes the instability

For y=Xβ+ϵy=X\beta+\epsilon , ordinary least squares minimizes ∥y−Xβ∥22\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2 . If X⊤XX^\top X is invertible,

β̂OLS=(X⊤X)−1X⊤y.\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.

When columns are highly correlated, different coefficient combinations can make nearly the same prediction on the observed data. The loss then changes little along some directions, and small sample perturbations can produce large coefficient changes. If the design is rank deficient, the inverse expression does not apply; the least-squares solution may not be unique.

Coefficient penalties depend on measurement units. Standardizing predictors avoids penalizing the same physical signal differently simply because its units changed from dollars to millions of dollars. Fit any preprocessing on the training data used by the procedure.

Add an L2 preference

Ridge adds a squared coefficient penalty:

β̂Ridge∈argminβ[∥y−Xβ∥22+λ∥β∥22].\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Ridge}}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_\beta \left[\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_2^2\right].

Differentiating gives −2X⊤(y−Xβ)+2λβ-2X^\top(y-X\beta)+2\lambda\beta . Set this to zero:

(X⊤X+λI)β̂=X⊤y,(X^\top X+\lambda I)\hat\beta=X^\top y,

so

β̂Ridge=(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y.\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Ridge}}=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.

For positive λ\lambda , adding λI\lambda I lifts the weak eigenvalues. The intercept is usually not penalized; centering the data or handling the intercept separately keeps this matrix expression consistent with that choice.

Step by step Derive OLS and Ridge from individual coefficient derivatives

Setting. Let X∈ℝn×pX\in\mathbb R^{n\times p} , β∈ℝp\beta\in\mathbb R^p , and y∈ℝny\in\mathbb R^n . Center columns and targets so the intercept can be handled separately. Use the sum-loss convention

J(β)=12∑i=1n(yi−∑k=1pXikβk)2+λ2∑k=1pβk2,λ≥0.J(\beta)=\frac12\sum_{i=1}^n\left(y_i-\sum_{k=1}^pX_{ik}\beta_k\right)^2+\frac\lambda2\sum_{k=1}^p\beta_k^2,\qquad\lambda\ge0.

1 · Differentiate one residual

Define ri=yi−∑kXikβkr_i=y_i-\sum_kX_{ik}\beta_k . Only the term with k=jk=j changes with βj\beta_j , so ∂ri/∂βj=−Xij\partial r_i/\partial\beta_j=-X_{ij} . The chain rule gives

∂(ri2/2)∂βj=ri(−Xij).\frac{\partial(r_i^2/2)}{\partial\beta_j}=r_i(-X_{ij}).

The penalty derivative is ∂(λ∑kβk2/2)/∂βj=λβj\partial(\lambda\sum_k\beta_k^2/2)/\partial\beta_j=\lambda\beta_j .

2 · Add the observation contributions

∂J∂βj=−∑iXij(yi−∑kXikβk)+λβj.\frac{\partial J}{\partial\beta_j}=-\sum_iX_{ij}\left(y_i-\sum_kX_{ik}\beta_k\right)+\lambda\beta_j.

Collecting these pp coordinates gives

∇βJ=−X⊤(y−Xβ)+λβ.\nabla_\beta J=-X^\top(y-X\beta)+\lambda\beta.

3 · Set the gradient to zero and collect coefficients

−X⊤y+X⊤Xβ+λβ=0,-X^\top y+X^\top X\beta+\lambda\beta=0,
(X⊤X+λI)β=X⊤y.(X^\top X+\lambda I)\beta=X^\top y.

If λ>0\lambda>0 , the matrix is positive definite: for any nonzero vv ,

v⊤(X⊤X+λI)v=∥Xv∥2+λ∥v∥2>0.v^\top(X^\top X+\lambda I)v=\|Xv\|^2+\lambda\|v\|^2>0.

Thus the stationary point is the unique global minimum and

β̂λ=(X⊤X+λI)−1X⊤y.\hat\beta_\lambda=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.

Numerically, solve this linear system rather than explicitly forming its inverse.

4 · Recover OLS and handle rank deficiency

Set λ=0\lambda=0 . If XX has full column rank, X⊤XX^\top X is invertible and the OLS formula follows. Otherwise the normal equations still characterize minimizers, but there may be infinitely many coefficient vectors; X+yX^+y selects the minimum-Euclidean-norm solution.

5 · Track normalization and the intercept

For ∥y−Xβ∥2/(2n)+λ∥β∥2/2\|y-X\beta\|^2/(2n)+\lambda\|\beta\|^2/2 , the system is (X⊤X+nλI)β=X⊤y(X^\top X+n\lambda I)\beta=X^\top y . The same symbol λ\lambda therefore has a different numerical scale. With an unpenalized intercept, differentiation gives b̂=y‾−x‾⊤β̂\hat b=\bar y-\bar x^\top\hat\beta ; centering is what made it disappear above.

The spectral explanation

Let X=UDV⊤X=UDV^\top , with singular values djd_j . In the identifiable singular directions,

β̂Ridge=Vdiag(djdj2+λ)U⊤y.\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Ridge}}=V\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right)U^\top y.

OLS uses the factor 1/dj1/d_j . Relative to OLS, Ridge attenuates direction jj by

dj2dj2+λ.\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}.

This factor is close to one when dj2d_j^2 is large relative to λ\lambda , and close to zero when the direction is weakly identified. The formula explains why Ridge is not simply “subtracting the same amount from every coefficient.” Shrinkage depends on the geometry of the design.

Step by step Derive the spectral shrinkage factors and the constraint connection

Setting. Use the sum-loss Ridge objective above. Take a thin SVD over the rr positive singular values: X=UrDrVr⊤X=U_rD_rV_r^\top , with Dr=diag(d1,…,dr)D_r=\operatorname{diag}(d_1,\ldots,d_r) and dj>0d_j>0 . Both UrU_r and VrV_r have orthonormal columns.

1 · Express the normal equations in singular directions

Since X⊤X=VrDr2Vr⊤X^\top X=V_rD_r^2V_r^\top and X⊤y=VrDrUr⊤yX^\top y=V_rD_rU_r^\top y , write the coefficient as β=Vra+β⊥\beta=V_ra+\beta_\perp , where Vr⊤β⊥=0V_r^\top\beta_\perp=0 . The data-fit term cannot see β⊥\beta_\perp , while λ>0\lambda>0 penalizes it, so the minimizer has β⊥=0\beta_\perp=0 .

2 · Solve one coordinate at a time

Premultiply the normal equations by Vr⊤V_r^\top :

(Dr2+λI)a=DrUr⊤y.(D_r^2+\lambda I)a=D_rU_r^\top y.

If cj=uj⊤yc_j=u_j^\top y , this says (dj2+λ)aj=djcj(d_j^2+\lambda)a_j=d_jc_j . Therefore

aj=djdj2+λcj,β̂λ=Vrdiag(djdj2+λ)Ur⊤y.a_j=\frac{d_j}{d_j^2+\lambda}c_j,\qquad \hat\beta_\lambda=V_r\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{d_j}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right)U_r^\top y.

3 · Compare coefficients and fitted values with OLS

The minimum-norm OLS coordinate is ajOLS=cj/dja_j^{\mathrm{OLS}}=c_j/d_j . Their ratio is

ajajOLS=dj2dj2+λ\frac{a_j}{a_j^{\mathrm{OLS}}}=\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}

when the denominator coordinate is nonzero; the same multiplier identity holds even when cj=0c_j=0 . Multiplying by XX yields

ŷλ=Urdiag(dj2dj2+λ)Ur⊤y.\hat y_\lambda=U_r\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{d_j^2}{d_j^2+\lambda}\right)U_r^\top y.

Small dj2d_j^2 receive the strongest shrinkage. Original-feature coefficients mix singular directions, so an individual βk\beta_k need not decrease monotonically in magnitude.

4 · Check the orthogonal example

If X⊤X=IX^\top X=I , all singular values are one and β̂λ=β̂OLS/(1+λ)\hat\beta_\lambda=\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}/(1+\lambda) . For (3,−2,0.5)(3,-2,0.5) and λ=1\lambda=1 , divide each coordinate by two to get (1.5,−1,0.25)(1.5,-1,0.25) .

5 · Connect to a constrained problem without assuming a one-to-one map

For minimizing ∥y−Xβ∥2/2\|y-X\beta\|^2/2 subject to ∥β∥2≤t\|\beta\|^2\le t , the Lagrangian is

ℒ(β,ν)=12∥y−Xβ∥2+ν(∥β∥2−t),ν≥0.\mathcal L(\beta,\nu)=\frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2+\nu(\|\beta\|^2-t),\qquad\nu\ge0.

Stationarity gives X⊤(Xβ−y)+2νβ=0X^\top(X\beta-y)+2\nu\beta=0 , the Ridge equation with λ=2ν\lambda=2\nu . Complementary slackness requires ν(∥β∥2−t)=0\nu(\|\beta\|^2-t)=0 . When a constraint is inactive, several tt values can share the same unregularized solution. Equivalence of appropriate solutions does not imply a globally one-to-one correspondence at every endpoint.

Work the orthogonal case

Assume X⊤X=IX^\top X=I and take β̂OLS=(3,−2,0.5)⊤\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}=(3,-2,0.5)^\top. Ridge then reduces to

β̂Ridge=11+λβ̂OLS.\hat\beta_{\mathrm{Ridge}}=\frac{1}{1+\lambda}\hat\beta_{\mathrm{OLS}}.

For λ=1\lambda=1 , the coefficients become (1.5,−1,0.25)⊤(1.5,-1,0.25)^\top . They shrink continuously rather than being thresholded to exact zeros. This particular uniform shrinkage holds because the design is orthogonal with the stated normalization.

Geometry and coefficient paths

The constrained interpretation minimizes fitting error within an L2 ball. In two dimensions, that feasible region is a circle. By contrast, the L1 ball has corners lying on coordinate axes, which is central to the sparsity argument in the next chapter.

Spectral shrinkage acts along the singular directions of the design matrix. With correlated predictors, a coefficient in the original coordinate system need not decrease monotonically in magnitude as the penalty grows. Uniform coordinate-wise shrinkage holds in the orthogonal example because all design directions have the same normalization.

From linear models to neural weights

For neural weights, an L2 penalty has the form λ2∑ℓ∥W[ℓ]∥F2\tfrac\lambda2\sum_\ell\lVert W^{[\ell]}\rVert_F^2, whose gradient adds λW\lambda W to the data gradient. The Frobenius norm sums squared matrix entries. Biases or particular parameter tensors may be treated separately by design.

For basic SGD, this produces a multiplicative shrinkage factor. For adaptive optimizers, the distinction between adding a penalty gradient and applying decoupled decay matters; see AdamW . Always keep the loss normalization and penalty normalization explicit when comparing derivations, libraries, or batch reductions.

Check the regularization identities Local worked example · Python / PyTorch

Numerical checks of orthogonal Ridge, soft thresholding, and inverted dropout, plus a seeded Monte Carlo check of the Gaussian input-noise identity. Run the script locally.

regularization_identities.py 
"""Small numerical checks for regularization identities from the supplied guide"""

import torch


def main() -> None:
    torch.set_num_threads(1)
    generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(41)
    dtype = torch.float64

    # Orthogonal Ridge example with the source's unnormalized objective
    x = torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype)
    y = torch.tensor([3.0, -2.0, 0.5], dtype=dtype)
    ridge = torch.linalg.solve(x.T @ x + torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype), x.T @ y)
    torch.testing.assert_close(ridge, torch.tensor([1.5, -1.0, 0.25], dtype=dtype))
    print("Orthogonal Ridge coefficients:", ridge.tolist())

    # The LASSO soft threshold is exact for this orthogonal scalar subproblem
    z = torch.tensor([2.4, 0.5, -1.1], dtype=dtype)
    lasso = z.sign() * (z.abs() - 0.7).clamp_min(0)
    torch.testing.assert_close(lasso, torch.tensor([1.7, 0.0, -0.4], dtype=dtype))
    print("Soft-thresholded coefficients:", lasso.tolist())

    # Verify the Gaussian input-noise identity approximately by Monte Carlo
    x = torch.randn(5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    beta = torch.tensor([0.6, -0.3, 0.8], dtype=dtype)
    y = x @ beta + torch.tensor([0.1, -0.2, 0.3, -0.1, 0.2], dtype=dtype)
    sigma = 0.2
    perturbations = sigma * torch.randn(100_000, 5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    noisy_losses = (y - (x + perturbations) @ beta).square().sum(dim=1)
    empirical = noisy_losses.mean()
    analytical = (y - x @ beta).square().sum() + len(x) * sigma**2 * beta.square().sum()
    standard_error = noisy_losses.std() / noisy_losses.numel()**0.5
    assert (empirical - analytical).abs() < 6 * standard_error
    print(f"Expected noisy squared loss: {analytical.item():.6f}")
    print(f"Monte Carlo estimate: {empirical.item():.6f} ± {standard_error.item():.6f} SE")

    # Reproduce the fixed-mask inverted-dropout example
    h = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, -3.0, 4.0], dtype=dtype)
    mask = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype)
    masked = mask * h / 0.5
    torch.testing.assert_close(masked, torch.tensor([4.0, 0.0, -6.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype))
    print("Inverted dropout under the illustrated mask:", masked.tolist())
    print("Exact checks passed; the noise expectation is a finite-sample approximation")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Check your understanding Why does Ridge help when predictors are correlated?

Correlated predictors create weakly identified singular directions and small eigenvalues of XᵀX. Ridge adds λI and attenuates those unstable directions relative to OLS. It trades unbiased fitting of those directions for more stable estimates.
