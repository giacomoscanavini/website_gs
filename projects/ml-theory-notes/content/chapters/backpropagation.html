Local derivatives become a global sensitivity

Backpropagation is the chain rule organized so that intermediate results are reused. For the scalar path w→z→a→Lw\to z\to a\to L ,

∂L∂w=∂L∂a∂a∂z∂z∂w.\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}\frac{\partial z}{\partial w}.

If z=wx+bz=wx+b , a=ReLU(z)a=\operatorname{ReLU}(z) , and L=12(a−y)2L=\tfrac12(a-y)^2 , then an active unit gives (a−y)x(a-y)x for the weight gradient. An inactive unit gives zero through this path. The derivative of the affine part says how the parameter changes the pre-activation; the activation derivative says how that change survives the nonlinearity; the loss derivative says whether it helps the objective.

Step by step Apply the chain rule to every scalar dependency

Setting. z=wx+bz=wx+b , a=ϕ(z)a=\phi(z) , and L=12(a−y)2L=\tfrac12(a-y)^2 . Treat xx and yy as constants when differentiating the parameters.

1 · Differentiate the loss with respect to its direct input

∂L∂a=122(a−y)=a−y.\frac{\partial L}{\partial a}=\frac12\,2(a-y)=a-y.

This is not yet the weight gradient: the weight reaches the loss through zz and aa .

2 · Differentiate the activation and affine map

∂a∂z=ϕ′(z),∂z∂w=x,∂z∂b=1,∂z∂x=w.\frac{\partial a}{\partial z}=\phi'(z),\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial w}=x,\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial b}=1,\quad \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}=w.

3 · Multiply along each path

∂L∂w=(a−y)ϕ′(z)x,\frac{\partial L}{\partial w}=(a-y)\phi'(z)x,
∂L∂b=(a−y)ϕ′(z),∂L∂x=(a−y)ϕ′(z)w.\frac{\partial L}{\partial b}=(a-y)\phi'(z),\qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial x}=(a-y)\phi'(z)w.

The input derivative is useful for propagating sensitivity into an earlier layer even when xx is not a trainable parameter.

4 · Evaluate the ReLU branches

For z>0z>0 , a=za=z and ϕ′(z)=1\phi'(z)=1 , so the weight gradient is (z−y)x(z-y)x . For z<0z<0 , ϕ′(z)=0\phi'(z)=0 , and all three path derivatives above are zero. At z=0z=0 , specify the software convention rather than using a nonexistent classical derivative.

5 · Add derivatives when a variable feeds multiple branches

If aa feeds two paths u(a)u(a) and v(a)v(a) that both enter LL , a perturbation dada changes both:

dL=∂L∂ududada+rac∂L∂vdvdada.dL=\frac{\partial L}{\partial u}\frac{du}{da}da+ rac{\partial L}{\partial v}\frac{dv}{da}da.

Divide by dada to get the sum of both contributions. Backpropagation multiplies along a path and adds across paths ; ignoring the addition loses valid dependencies.

The matrix recurrence

Define a layer’s error signal as a derivative, not a new trainable variable:

δ[ℓ]=∂L∂z[ℓ].\delta^{[\ell]}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial z^{[\ell]}}.

The guide’s single-example, column-vector equations are

∇W[ℓ]L=δ[ℓ](a[ℓ−1])⊤,∇b[ℓ]L=δ[ℓ],\nabla_{W^{[\ell]}}L=\delta^{[\ell]}(a^{[\ell-1]})^\top,\qquad \nabla_{b^{[\ell]}}L=\delta^{[\ell]},
δ[ℓ]=(W[ℓ+1])⊤δ[ℓ+1]⊙ϕ′(z[ℓ]).\delta^{[\ell]}=(W^{[\ell+1]})^\top\delta^{[\ell+1]}\odot\phi'(z^{[\ell]}).

The outer product gives exactly the shape of the weight matrix. Its entry (j,k)(j,k) is “sensitivity at output unit jj times input activation kk .” This is a useful way to recover the formula rather than memorize it. The Hadamard product ⊙\odot applies the activation derivative coordinate by coordinate.

Step by step Recover matrix and mini-batch gradients from individual entries

Setting. One layer uses a∈ℝna\in\mathbb R^n , W∈ℝm×nW\in\mathbb R^{m\times n} , b,z∈ℝmb,z\in\mathbb R^m , with z=Wa+bz=Wa+b . Let δj=∂L/∂zj\delta_j=\partial L/\partial z_j . Vectors of sensitivities are columns.

1 · Differentiate one weight entry

Since zi=∑kWikak+biz_i=\sum_kW_{ik}a_k+b_i ,

∂zi∂Wjk={aki=j,0i≠j.\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial W_{jk}}=\begin{cases}a_k&i=j,\\0&i\ne j.\end{cases}

The multivariable chain rule sums all output paths:

∂L∂Wjk=∑i∂L∂zi∂zi∂Wjk=δjak.\frac{\partial L}{\partial W_{jk}}=\sum_i\frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}\frac{\partial z_i}{\partial W_{jk}}=\delta_j a_k.

Those entries form the outer product ∇WL=δa⊤\nabla_WL=\delta a^\top , whose shape is m×nm\times n .

2 · Differentiate a bias and an input entry

Only zjz_j contains bjb_j , with derivative one, so ∂L/∂bj=δj\partial L/\partial b_j=\delta_j . In contrast, input aka_k influences every output:

∂L∂ak=∑jδjWjk.\frac{\partial L}{\partial a_k}=\sum_j\delta_jW_{jk}.

This is coordinate kk of W⊤δW^\top\delta , hence ∇aL=W⊤δ\nabla_aL=W^\top\delta . The transpose is not an inverse.

3 · Cross the preceding activation

If ak=ϕ(sk)a_k=\phi(s_k) independently per coordinate, then

∂L∂sk=(W⊤δ)kϕ′(sk).\frac{\partial L}{\partial s_k}=(W^\top\delta)_k\phi'(s_k).

Collecting coordinates gives (W⊤δ)⊙ϕ′(s)(W^\top\delta)\odot\phi'(s) . Applying this repeatedly yields the hidden-layer recurrence in the chapter.

4 · Sum a batch without losing its normalization

Let rows of A∈ℝB×nA\in\mathbb R^{B\times n} be layer inputs and rows of Δ∈ℝB×m\Delta\in\mathbb R^{B\times m} be per-example derivatives ∂ℓi/∂zi\partial\ell_i/\partial z_i . For LB=B−1∑iℓiL_B=B^{-1}\sum_i\ell_i ,

∇WLB=1B∑iδiai⊤=1BΔ⊤A,\nabla_WL_B=\frac1B\sum_i\delta_i a_i^\top=\frac1B\Delta^\top A,
∇bLB=1BΔ⊤𝟏B,∇ALB=1BΔW.\nabla_bL_B=\frac1B\Delta^\top\mathbf1_B,\qquad \nabla_AL_B=\frac1B\Delta W.

The shape checks are (m×B)(B×n)(m\times B)(B\times n) , (m×B)(B×1)(m\times B)(B\times1) , and (B×m)(m×n)(B\times m)(m\times n) .

5 · Do not average twice

If D=∂LB/∂ZD=\partial L_B/\partial Z already includes 1/B1/B , then D=Δ/BD=\Delta/B . The same results are D⊤AD^\top A , D⊤𝟏BD^\top\mathbf1_B , and DWDW —with no additional division . These separable-example expressions assume the layer’s inputs are the quantities being differentiated; batch-coupled operations such as training-mode BatchNorm need their own Jacobian when propagating further backward.

Step 3: send sensitivity into the hidden layer

Both hidden ReLUs are active, so their derivative vector is (1,1)⊤(1,1)^\top . With output weights (0.7,−0.5)(0.7,-0.5) ,

δ[1]=[0.7−0.5](−0.636453)≈[−0.4455170.318226].\delta^{[1]}=\begin{bmatrix}0.7\\-0.5\end{bmatrix}(-0.636453) \approx\begin{bmatrix}-0.445517\\0.318226\end{bmatrix}.

The two hidden units receive opposite signs because their outgoing weights have opposite signs. Increasing one hidden activation raises the logit, while increasing the other lowers it. Backpropagation accounts for that structure automatically.

Step 4: first-layer gradients

Using x⊤=(1,2)x^\top=(1,2) ,

∇W[1]L=δ[1]x⊤≈[−0.445517−0.8910340.3182260.636453],\nabla_{W^{[1]}}L=\delta^{[1]}x^\top \approx\begin{bmatrix}-0.445517&-0.891034\\0.318226&0.636453\end{bmatrix},

Every parameter gradient has now been computed. No parameter has yet been updated. In particular, the hidden derivative used the old output weights, because all gradients describe the same forward computation.

Why the algorithm is efficient

A naive finite-difference procedure perturbs parameters one at a time and repeatedly reevaluates the loss. Reverse-mode differentiation instead starts from the scalar objective and reuses sensitivities as it traverses the graph backward. This is particularly appropriate for a function with many parameters and one scalar loss.

For a mini-batch mean loss, the parameter gradients are the mean of the per-example gradients. Batch shape conventions change the matrix arrangement, not the chain-rule argument. In PyTorch, .backward() carries out the derivative calculation; .step() applies the chosen update. Backpropagation is not an alternative to Adam: they act at different stages.

Reproduce the full forward and backward pass Local worked example · Python / PyTorch

A small authored companion reproducing the exact network in the supplied guide. Every parameter gradient is checked against the hand calculation, followed by one update. Run locally; Python is not executed by this webpage.

worked_backprop.py 
"""Reproduce the supplied 2 → 2 → 1 forward and backward calculation"""

import torch
import torch.nn.functional as F


def main() -> None:
    dtype = torch.float64
    x = torch.tensor([1.0, 2.0], dtype=dtype)
    y = torch.tensor(1.0, dtype=dtype)
    w1 = torch.tensor([[0.5, -0.2], [0.3, 0.8]], dtype=dtype, requires_grad=True)
    b1 = torch.tensor([0.1, -0.1], dtype=dtype, requires_grad=True)
    w2 = torch.tensor([0.7, -0.5], dtype=dtype, requires_grad=True)
    b2 = torch.tensor(0.2, dtype=dtype, requires_grad=True)

    z1 = w1 @ x + b1
    hidden = z1.relu()
    logit = w2 @ hidden + b2
    loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(logit, y)
    loss.backward()

    # The hand calculation uses the source's single-observation convention
    with torch.no_grad():
        delta2 = logit.sigmoid() - y
        delta1 = w2 * delta2 * (z1 > 0)
        expected = [torch.outer(delta1, x), delta1, delta2 * hidden, delta2]
        parameters = [w1, b1, w2, b2]
        for parameter, gradient in zip(parameters, expected):
            torch.testing.assert_close(parameter.grad, gradient, rtol=1e-12, atol=1e-12)

        print(f"Hidden activations: {hidden.tolist()}")
        print(f"Logit: {logit.item():.6f}; probability: {logit.sigmoid().item():.6f}")
        print(f"Loss before update: {loss.item():.6f}")
        for name, parameter in zip(["W1", "b1", "W2", "b2"], parameters):
            print(f"Gradient {name}: {parameter.grad.tolist()}")
            parameter.add_(parameter.grad, alpha=-0.1)

        next_logit = w2 @ (w1 @ x + b1).relu() + b2
        next_loss = F.binary_cross_entropy_with_logits(next_logit, y)
        assert next_loss < loss, "The illustrated update should reduce this example's loss"
        print(f"Updated W1: {w1.tolist()}")
        print(f"Updated W2: {w2.tolist()}")
        print(f"Loss after update: {next_loss.item():.6f}")
        print("All four autograd gradients match the hand derivation")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Check your understanding What is the role of the transpose in the backward pass?

Forward propagation maps earlier activations to later pre-activations using W. The derivative of that map sends a later sensitivity back using Wᵀ. The transpose redistributes the loss sensitivity across the incoming coordinates; it is not a matrix inverse.
