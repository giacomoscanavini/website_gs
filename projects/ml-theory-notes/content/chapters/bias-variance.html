Generalization is not the same as fitting

The regularization guide asks how to constrain, perturb, average, or stop a learning algorithm so that it behaves better on unseen data. Its starting observation is that flexible models can fit accidental sample structure alongside stable signal.

A penalty makes this preference explicit:

θ̂∈argminθ[1n∑i=1nℓi(θ)+λΩ(θ)].\hat\theta\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_\theta\left[\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n\ell_i(\theta)+\lambda\Omega(\theta)\right].

A large penalty discourages a region of parameter space; it does not prove that the retained functions are the correct ones. The statistical usefulness depends on whether the preference matches the problem.

The squared-error decomposition

At a fixed input xx , write Y=f(x)+ϵY=f(x)+\epsilon . Under the usual zero-mean noise assumptions, the source gives

𝔼D,ϵ[(Y−f̂D(x))2]=σϵ2+(𝔼Df̂D(x)−f(x))2+VarD(f̂D(x)).\mathbb E_{D,\epsilon}\left[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2\right] =\sigma_\epsilon^2+\left(\mathbb E_D\hat f_D(x)-f(x)\right)^2+ \operatorname{Var}_D\left(\hat f_D(x)\right).

The dataset DD is random in this thought experiment: imagine repeatedly collecting a training set and refitting the procedure. Bias is the distance between the average fitted prediction and the true function. Variance measures how much the fitted prediction changes across those datasets. Irreducible noise is uncertainty in the outcome even if the function were known.

An expanded derivation adds and subtracts 𝔼Df̂D(x)\mathbb E_D\hat f_D(x) inside the squared error. The centered estimator term has expectation zero; with the stated noise assumptions, the cross terms vanish. The remaining terms are precisely noise, squared bias, and estimator variance. This is a decomposition for squared-error prediction, not an identical additive formula for every classification metric.

The training sample DD is independent of this new outcome given xx . Expectations over DD also include any randomization used to fit the model.

1 · Insert the mean fitted prediction

Let μ(x)=𝔼D[f̂D(x)]\mu(x)=\mathbb E_D[\hat f_D(x)] . Add and subtract μ(x)\mu(x) :

Y−f̂D(x)=ε+[f(x)−μ(x)]+[μ(x)−f̂D(x)].Y-\hat f_D(x)=\varepsilon+[f(x)-\mu(x)]+[\mu(x)-\hat f_D(x)].

The middle term is fixed once xx and the training procedure are fixed. The final term varies with the training sample and has mean zero.

Abbreviate b=f(x)−μ(x)b=f(x)-\mu(x) and v=μ(x)−f̂D(x)v=\mu(x)-\hat f_D(x) . Then

Because bb is fixed and 𝔼[ε]=0\mathbb E[\varepsilon]=0 , 𝔼[εb]=0\mathbb E[\varepsilon b]=0 . Because 𝔼D[v]=μ−𝔼D[f̂D]=0\mathbb E_D[v]=\mu-\mathbb E_D[\hat f_D]=0 , 𝔼D[bv]=0\mathbb E_D[bv]=0 . Finally, independence of the new noise and training sample gives 𝔼[εv]=𝔼[ε]𝔼D[v]=0\mathbb E[\varepsilon v]=\mathbb E[\varepsilon]\mathbb E_D[v]=0 .

4 · Recognize the three remaining expectations

𝔼D,ε[(Y−f̂D(x))2]=σ2(x)+[μ(x)−f(x)]2+𝔼D[(f̂D(x)−μ(x))2].\mathbb E_{D,\varepsilon}[(Y-\hat f_D(x))^2]=\sigma^2(x)+[\mu(x)-f(x)]^2+\mathbb E_D[(\hat f_D(x)-\mu(x))^2].

The last term is the variance of the fitted prediction. The squared middle term is squared bias; its sign does not matter because it is squared.

5 · State the scope of the identity

This is an identity for squared error at a fixed input, assuming the stated moments exist. Integrating it over a test-input distribution gives an average-risk decomposition. It is not the same formula for zero–one classification error, and it is not valid to treat training residuals as independent new-outcome errors. Increasing regularization does not force every individual term to change monotonically in every model.

Why accepting bias can reduce total error

An unregularized estimator can respond strongly to a small change in the training sample. Shrinkage can move its average prediction away from the true function slightly while reducing its fluctuations more substantially. Expected test error can then fall even though training fit worsens.

That explains why lower training error is not a reliable comparison criterion for regularized models. Conversely, if the model already misses important signal, adding stronger penalties may increase bias without a useful variance reduction.

There is no single “regularization knob” that captures all of these. Two interventions can be complementary, or jointly so strong that useful structure is never learned.

A data-dependent diagnosis

The source describes strong training performance and weak validation as the classic overfitting pattern. Weak performance on both can instead indicate underfitting, optimization trouble, inadequate inputs, or excessive regularization.

Selected LASSO features changing across folds can indicate collinearity and weak identification rather than failure of the optimizer. An ensemble that barely improves can indicate highly correlated component errors. These observations suggest different remedies because they arise from different statistical structures.

Why validation belongs inside the explanation

Choosing λ\lambda , dropout rate, augmentation strength, stopping time, or ensemble weights changes the learning procedure. The source explicitly warns against selecting them using the final test set. For temporal data, chronology and the relevant prediction horizon constrain both validation and resampling.

The point is not that one penalty is universally best. It is to make the preference explicit, identify which failure it is intended to reduce, and evaluate the complete selected procedure on data that genuinely plays the role of unseen data.

Check your understanding Does regularization always improve generalization?

No. It imposes preferences that may reduce sensitivity to the sample but can also exclude useful signal or stop learning too early. Under squared loss, the familiar intuition is accepting some additional bias for a larger variance reduction. Excessive regularization can make both training and validation poor.
