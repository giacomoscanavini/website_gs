LASSO, subgradients, and exact zeros

LASSO combines coefficient shrinkage with variable selection. The subgradient of the absolute-value penalty allows a range of loss gradients to be balanced at an exactly zero coefficient.

The objective changes the preference

LASSO minimizes

β̂∈argminβ[12∥y−Xβ∥22+λ∥β∥1],∥β∥1=∑j|βj|.\hat\beta\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_\beta\left[\frac12\lVert y-X\beta\rVert_2^2+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1\right],\qquad \lVert\beta\rVert_1=\sum_j|\beta_j|.

Like Ridge, it discourages large coefficients. Its distinguishing feature is that some coefficients can become exactly zero , providing variable selection as well as shrinkage.

The reason is not merely that absolute values are “stronger” than squares. The shape at zero changes the optimality condition.

The kink and the subgradient

For an individual coefficient,

∂|β|={{1}β>0β=0{−1}β<0.\partial|\beta|=\begin{cases}\{1\}&\beta>0\\[-1,1]&\beta=0\\\{-1\}&\beta<0.\end{cases}

At zero, the penalty’s subgradient is an interval. A small enough data-loss derivative can be balanced by a member of this interval while keeping the coefficient at zero. L2’s smooth derivative does not produce the same zero-valued interval of solutions.

The geometric picture is the L1 diamond. A smooth loss contour often first touches it at a corner, where a coordinate is zero. This is an intuition for the algebra, not a substitute for the conditions of a particular problem.

Derive soft thresholding

For a standardized orthogonal one-coordinate problem,

minβ12(β−z)2+λ|β|,\min_\beta\ \frac12(\beta-z)^2+\lambda|\beta|,

consider the positive branch. Differentiation gives β−z+λ=0\beta-z+\lambda=0 , so a positive solution is β=z−λ\beta=z-\lambda , valid only when z>λz>\lambda . On the negative branch, β−z−λ=0\beta-z-\lambda=0 , giving β=z+λ\beta=z+\lambda , valid when z<−λz<-\lambda . For |z|≤λ|z|\le\lambda , zero satisfies the subgradient condition.

Combining the cases,

β̂=soft(z,λ)=sign(z)max(|z|−λ,0).\hat\beta=\operatorname{soft}(z,\lambda) =\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0).

This derivation makes the difference from hard thresholding clear. Values above the threshold are still shrunk; they are not left unchanged.

1 · Obtain the subgradient of absolute value at zero

A subgradient ss at zero must satisfy |u|≥|0|+s(u−0)=su|u|\ge |0|+s(u-0)=su for every real uu . For positive uu , divide by uu to obtain s≤1s\le1 . When b>0b>0 , F′(b)=b−z+λF'(b)=b-z+\lambda . Setting it to zero gives b=z−λb=z-\lambda . This belongs to the assumed positive branch only when z>λz>\lambda .

3 · Solve the negative branch

When b<0b<0 , F′(b)=b−z−λF'(b)=b-z-\lambda . Setting it to zero gives b=z+λb=z+\lambda , which is negative only when z<−λz<-\lambda .

At b=0b=0 , stationarity means 0∈−z+λ[−1,1]0\in-z+\lambda[-1,1] . For λ>0\lambda>0 this is equivalent to z/λ∈[−1,1]z/\lambda\in[-1,1] , or |z|≤λ|z|\le\lambda . The λ=0\lambda=0 case reduces to minimizing a quadratic, with solution b=zb=z .

5 · Assemble the unique minimizer

b*={z−λz>λ,0|z|≤λ,z+λz<−λ,=sign(z)max(|z|−λ,0).b^*=\begin{cases}z-\lambda&z>\lambda,\\0&|z|\le\lambda,\\z+\lambda&z<-\lambda,\end{cases} =\operatorname{sign}(z)\max(|z|-\lambda,0).

For λ=0.7\lambda=0.7 , the inputs 2.42.4 , 0.50.5 , and −1.1-1.1 produce 1.71.7 , 00 , and −0.4-0.4 . At z=±λz=\pm\lambda , both adjacent formulas meet at zero.

6 · Relate the result to an optimization step

A proximal-gradient step on f(β)+λ∥β∥1f(\beta)+\lambda\|\beta\|_1 first sets z=β−η∇f(β)z=\beta-\eta\nabla f(\beta) , then minimizes ∥b−z∥2/(2η)+λ∥b∥1\|b-z\|^2/(2\eta)+\lambda\|b\|_1 . Multiplying by η\eta shows that each coordinate uses threshold ηλ\eta\lambda . An arbitrary SGD or Adam step on an L1 subgradient is not the same operation and need not produce exact zeros.

Coordinate descent and scaling conventions

A coordinate-descent update forms a residual excluding feature jj , then solves a one-coordinate penalized problem. For a mean squared-error objective ∥y−Xβ∥2/(2n)+λ∥β∥1\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2/(2n)+\lambda\lVert\beta\rVert_1 , one convenient form is

βj←soft(xj⊤r−j/n,λ)xj⊤xj/n.\beta_j\leftarrow \frac{\operatorname{soft}(x_j^\top r_{-j}/n,\lambda)}{x_j^\top x_j/n}.

The coordinate update above uses a mean-scaled fitting loss, unlike the summed loss at the beginning of the chapter. The numerical value of λ\lambda must change with that normalization to describe the same optimization problem.

Coordinate descent updates one coefficient at a time using its partial residual. The implementation example below plots LASSO and Elastic Net coefficient paths; the short Python companion checks the orthogonal soft-thresholding case.

Correlated predictors and interpretation

If two inputs nearly duplicate one another, LASSO may keep one and remove the other. Small changes in the sample can switch which is selected. A sparse coefficient vector therefore does not automatically identify a unique causal set of variables. It can be a compact predictive representation with unstable membership.

Under a compatible likelihood and maximum a posteriori formulation, a Laplace prior gives an L1 penalty and a Gaussian prior gives an L2 penalty. These are preferences over coefficients: a fitted zero does not prove that a variable has no causal role. Elastic Net combines both penalties to reduce some of the instability caused by correlated predictors.

Check the regularization identities Local worked example · Python / PyTorch

Numerical checks of orthogonal Ridge, soft thresholding, and inverted dropout, plus a seeded Monte Carlo check of the Gaussian input-noise identity. Run the script locally.

regularization_identities.py 
"""Small numerical checks for regularization identities from the supplied guide"""

import torch


def main() -> None:
    torch.set_num_threads(1)
    generator = torch.Generator().manual_seed(41)
    dtype = torch.float64

    # Orthogonal Ridge example with the source's unnormalized objective
    x = torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype)
    y = torch.tensor([3.0, -2.0, 0.5], dtype=dtype)
    ridge = torch.linalg.solve(x.T @ x + torch.eye(3, dtype=dtype), x.T @ y)
    torch.testing.assert_close(ridge, torch.tensor([1.5, -1.0, 0.25], dtype=dtype))
    print("Orthogonal Ridge coefficients:", ridge.tolist())

    # The LASSO soft threshold is exact for this orthogonal scalar subproblem
    z = torch.tensor([2.4, 0.5, -1.1], dtype=dtype)
    lasso = z.sign() * (z.abs() - 0.7).clamp_min(0)
    torch.testing.assert_close(lasso, torch.tensor([1.7, 0.0, -0.4], dtype=dtype))
    print("Soft-thresholded coefficients:", lasso.tolist())

    # Verify the Gaussian input-noise identity approximately by Monte Carlo
    x = torch.randn(5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    beta = torch.tensor([0.6, -0.3, 0.8], dtype=dtype)
    y = x @ beta + torch.tensor([0.1, -0.2, 0.3, -0.1, 0.2], dtype=dtype)
    sigma = 0.2
    perturbations = sigma * torch.randn(100_000, 5, 3, generator=generator, dtype=dtype)
    noisy_losses = (y - (x + perturbations) @ beta).square().sum(dim=1)
    empirical = noisy_losses.mean()
    analytical = (y - x @ beta).square().sum() + len(x) * sigma**2 * beta.square().sum()
    standard_error = noisy_losses.std() / noisy_losses.numel()**0.5
    assert (empirical - analytical).abs() < 6 * standard_error
    print(f"Expected noisy squared loss: {analytical.item():.6f}")
    print(f"Monte Carlo estimate: {empirical.item():.6f} ± {standard_error.item():.6f} SE")

    # Reproduce the fixed-mask inverted-dropout example
    h = torch.tensor([2.0, 1.0, -3.0, 4.0], dtype=dtype)
    mask = torch.tensor([1.0, 0.0, 1.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype)
    masked = mask * h / 0.5
    torch.testing.assert_close(masked, torch.tensor([4.0, 0.0, -6.0, 0.0], dtype=dtype))
    print("Inverted dropout under the illustrated mask:", masked.tolist())
    print("Exact checks passed; the noise expectation is a finite-sample approximation")


if __name__ == "__main__":
    main()

Check your understanding Why does L1 create exact zeros while L2 usually does not?

The absolute-value penalty has a subgradient interval at zero. A range of data-loss gradients can be balanced without moving the coefficient away from zero. Soft thresholding makes that interval explicit. L2 has a smooth derivative that continuously shrinks rather than creating the same threshold region.
