SGD, gradient noise, and learning rates

Stochastic gradient descent replaces the full training gradient with a mini-batch estimate. Batch size controls sampling noise; the learning rate controls how far each update moves.

Why move against the gradient?

A first-order expansion around the current parameters gives

J(θ+Δθ)≈J(θ)+∇J(θ)⊤Δθ.J(\theta+\Delta\theta)\approx J(\theta)+\nabla J(\theta)^\top\Delta\theta.

Among directions with the same Euclidean length, the negative gradient gives the largest local reduction in this linear approximation. Gradient descent therefore chooses

θt+1=θt−ηt∇J(θt).\theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_t\nabla J(\theta_t).

This is a local argument. A large step can leave the region where the linear approximation is useful, overshoot a low-loss region, or increase the objective. The gradient chooses a direction; the learning rate controls the distance.

Step by step Derive the negative-gradient direction and a safe quadratic step

Setting. JJ is differentiable at θ\theta with nonzero gradient gg . We first use the linear approximation and restrict the Euclidean step norm to at most r>0r>0 .

1 · Reduce the local problem to an inner product

The first-order model is J(θ+s)≈J(θ)+g⊤sJ(\theta+s)\approx J(\theta)+g^\top s . Since the first term is constant, choose ss minimizing g⊤sg^\top s subject to ∥s∥≤r\|s\|\le r .

2 · Apply Cauchy–Schwarz

g⊤s≥−∥g∥∥s∥≥−r∥g∥.g^\top s\ge-\|g\|\|s\|\ge-r\|g\|.

The first bound is attained when ss is opposite to gg , and the second when ∥s∥=r\|s\|=r . Therefore s*=−rg/∥g∥s_*=-rg/\|g\| . Parameterizing the distance as r=η∥g∥r=\eta\|g\| gives the familiar s=−ηgs=-\eta g .

3 · Bound the error of the linear model

Suppose the gradient is LL -Lipschitz along the step: ∥∇J(u)−∇J(v)∥≤L∥u−v∥\|\nabla J(u)-\nabla J(v)\|\le L\|u-v\| . Integrate the directional derivative:

J(θ+s)−J(θ)=∫01∇J(θ+ts)⊤sdt.J(\theta+s)-J(\theta)=\int_0^1\nabla J(\theta+ts)^\top s\,dt.

Add and subtract gg inside the integral. The remainder is at most

∫01Lt∥s∥2dt=L2∥s∥2.\int_0^1Lt\|s\|^2dt=\frac L2\|s\|^2.

Hence J(θ+s)≤J(θ)+g⊤s+(L/2)∥s∥2J(\theta+s)\le J(\theta)+g^\top s+(L/2)\|s\|^2 .

4 · Substitute the gradient step

J(θ−ηg)≤J(θ)−η(1−Lη2)∥g∥2.J(\theta-\eta g)\le J(\theta)-\eta\left(1-\frac{L\eta}{2}\right)\|g\|^2.

For g≠0g\ne0 and 0<η<2/L0<\eta<2/L , the upper bound is strictly lower than the old loss. This is a smooth, exact-gradient statement, not a guarantee for arbitrary stochastic updates.

5 · Check the simplest quadratic

For J(w)=aw2/2J(w)=aw^2/2 with a>0a>0 , g=awg=aw and wt+1=(1−ηa)wtw_{t+1}=(1-\eta a)w_t . The magnitude contracts exactly when |1−ηa|<1|1-\eta a|<1 , equivalent to 0<η<2/a0<\eta<2/a . If ηa>1\eta a>1 but below two, signs alternate while magnitudes shrink.

Replace an expensive average with a sampled average

The exact empirical gradient averages over all NN observations. A mini-batch BtB_t of size mm gives

gt=1m∑i∈Bt∇ℓi(θt),θt+1=θt−ηtgt.g_t=\frac1m\sum_{i\in B_t}\nabla\ell_i(\theta_t),\qquad \theta_{t+1}=\theta_t-\eta_t g_t.

For a fresh uniform batch drawn independently of the current optimization history, with per-observation gradients evaluated at the same parameters, the conditional estimate is unbiased:

𝔼[gt∣θt]=∇J(θt).\mathbb E[g_t\mid\theta_t]=\nabla J(\theta_t).

This does not say that each update points toward the exact minimum. Individual batches contain different observations and produce different directions. Write gt=∇J(θt)+ϵtg_t=\nabla J(\theta_t)+\epsilon_t to separate the average direction from gradient noise.

Batch size controls more than memory use

For independent draws of per-observation gradients at fixed parameters, covariance of their mean scales as 1/m1/m . Uniform sampling without replacement adds a finite-population correction; random reshuffling within an epoch needs a separate conditional argument. Larger batches therefore reduce gradient noise. But at fixed dataset size, they also reduce the number of updates made during one epoch.

For 60,000 observations and a batch size of 100, there are 600 updates per epoch. Doubling the batch size gives roughly half as many updates per pass, not merely a less noisy version of an otherwise identical run. Compare time, data passes, and update counts deliberately.

Gradient noise changes the optimization trajectory and can promote exploration. Excessive noise can also hinder fine convergence. A smaller batch is therefore not automatically better for either optimization or generalization.

Step by step Derive the mini-batch gradient’s expectation and variance

Setting. Fix a parameter vector θ\theta and its NN individual gradients gi=∇ℓi(θ)g_i=\nabla\ell_i(\theta) . Let g‾=N−1∑igi\bar g=N^{-1}\sum_i g_i and Σ=N−1∑i(gi−g‾)(gi−g‾)⊤\Sigma=N^{-1}\sum_i(g_i-\bar g)(g_i-\bar g)^\top . This population covariance uses denominator NN .

1 · Sample uniformly with replacement

For one random index II , P(I=i)=1/NP(I=i)=1/N , so 𝔼[gI]=∑igi/N=g‾\mathbb E[g_I]=\sum_i g_i/N=\bar g . For mm independent draws,

ĝ=1m∑j=1mgIj,𝔼[ĝ]=1m∑jg‾=g‾.\hat g=\frac1m\sum_{j=1}^m g_{I_j},\qquad\mathbb E[\hat g]=\frac1m\sum_j\bar g=\bar g.

2 · Expand covariance of the mean

Cov(ĝ)=1m2∑j,kCov(gIj,gIk).\operatorname{Cov}(\hat g)=\frac1{m^2}\sum_{j,k}\operatorname{Cov}(g_{I_j},g_{I_k}).

For j≠kj\ne k , independence makes each cross-covariance zero. There are mm diagonal terms equal to Σ\Sigma , giving Cov(ĝ)=Σ/m\operatorname{Cov}(\hat g)=\Sigma/m .

3 · Derive the without-replacement cross term

For two distinct uniform indices I≠JI\ne J , write ui=gi−g‾u_i=g_i-\bar g and note ∑iui=0\sum_i u_i=0 . Then

∑i≠juiuj⊤=(∑iui)(∑juj)⊤−∑iuiui⊤=−NΣ.\sum_{i\ne j}u_i u_j^\top=\left(\sum_i u_i\right)\left(\sum_j u_j\right)^\top-\sum_i u_i u_i^\top=-N\Sigma.

Dividing by N(N−1)N(N-1) gives Cov(gI,gJ)=−Σ/(N−1)\operatorname{Cov}(g_I,g_J)=-\Sigma/(N-1) .

4 · Substitute into the covariance sum

A batch of size m≤Nm\le N without replacement has mm diagonal and m(m−1)m(m-1) ordered off-diagonal terms:

Cov(ĝ)=1m2[mΣ−m(m−1)N−1Σ]=N−mm(N−1)Σ.\operatorname{Cov}(\hat g)=\frac1{m^2}\left[m\Sigma-\frac{m(m-1)}{N-1}\Sigma\right]=\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}\Sigma.

At m=Nm=N this is zero, as a full-dataset average must be.

5 · Condition on the actual training history

These unbiasedness statements hold for a fresh uniform draw independent of the current parameter choice, or at a fixed parameter vector. During a shuffled epoch, the next batch is drawn from the remaining observations, and the parameters depend on earlier batches. Conditional unbiasedness for the full gradient is not automatic. Correlated examples and batch-coupled losses also require a different covariance argument.

A numerical update

Take θt=(1.20,−0.50)⊤\theta_t=(1.20,-0.50)^\top, gt=(0.80,−0.30)⊤g_t=(0.80,-0.30)^\top, and η=0.1\eta=0.1. Thus

θt+1=[1.20−0.50]−0.1[0.80−0.30]=[1.12−0.47].\theta_{t+1}=\begin{bmatrix}1.20\\-0.50\end{bmatrix} -0.1\begin{bmatrix}0.80\\-0.30\end{bmatrix} =\begin{bmatrix}1.12\\-0.47\end{bmatrix}.

The negative second gradient causes the second parameter to increase. The update rule uses the sign of each sensitivity; it does not simply make weights smaller. Shrinkage requires a penalty or an explicit decay mechanism.

Schedules are not coordinate-wise adaptation

Common global learning-rate schedules include

ηt=η0γ⌊t/T⌋,ηt=η01+kt,ηt=η0e−kt,\eta_t=\eta_0\gamma^{\lfloor t/T\rfloor},\qquad \eta_t=\frac{\eta_0}{1+kt},\qquad \eta_t=\eta_0e^{-kt},

and cosine decay

ηt=ηmin+12(ηmax−ηmin)[1+cos(πtT)].\eta_t=\eta_{\min}+\frac12(\eta_{\max}-\eta_{\min})\left[1+\cos\left(\frac{\pi t}{T}\right)\right].

Each gives one scalar scale at time tt . AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam additionally alter the effective scale per coordinate using gradient history. A scheduled optimizer can use both mechanisms; they answer different questions.

The scheduling unit must be explicit. Here tt denotes an update index; implementations can step schedulers per epoch or per batch, depending on the intended design. Advancing a schedule at the wrong frequency changes the actual experiment.

What to tune and what to measure

A very small learning rate makes progress slow; a large one can destabilize training. Update scales differ across optimizers, so a comparison at one arbitrary shared learning rate is not necessarily fair. Tune the base rate and schedule for each method, and compare computational work as well as loss and validation performance.

Check your understanding Does using a larger batch always make training better?

It reduces variance of the gradient estimate under the usual sampling assumptions, but changes the number of updates per data pass and the computational trade-off. Lower gradient noise does not by itself guarantee faster wall-clock convergence or better generalization.
