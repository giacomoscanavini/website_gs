GNNs, graph attention, and relational bottlenecks

Graph neural networks combine information across connected nodes. Neighborhood aggregation, normalization, and permutation symmetry determine how node representations are updated.

The input includes relationships

A graph G=(V,E)G=(V,E) specifies entities and their relations. Node neighborhoods can vary in size, and the arbitrary ordering of nodes in a file should not change what a model means. A graph network therefore needs a rule for combining variable-size neighborhoods with appropriate permutation symmetry.

A message-passing layer can be written as

mi(ℓ)=AGGj∈𝒩(i)ψℓ(hi(ℓ),hj(ℓ),eij),m_i^{(\ell)}=\operatorname{AGG}_{j\in\mathcal N(i)} \psi_\ell(h_i^{(\ell)},h_j^{(\ell)},e_{ij}),
hi(ℓ+1)=ϕℓ(hi(ℓ),mi(ℓ)).h_i^{(\ell+1)}=\phi_\ell(h_i^{(\ell)},m_i^{(\ell)}).

A symmetric sum, mean, or max aggregator does not depend on the order in which neighbors are listed. Shared update functions then produce node outputs that reorder consistently when the nodes are permuted.

Normalized graph convolution

A common normalized GCN update is

Ã=A+I,D̃ii=∑jÃij,\tilde A=A+I,\qquad\tilde D_{ii}=\sum_j\tilde A_{ij},
H(ℓ+1)=σ(D̃−1/2ÃD̃−1/2H(ℓ)W(ℓ)).H^{(\ell+1)}=\sigma\left(\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}H^{(\ell)}W^{(\ell)}\right).

Self-loops include a node’s own representation. Degree normalization controls scale so high-degree neighborhoods do not dominate merely by size. The learned transformation and nonlinear activation then produce the next representation.

A simple mean-aggregation example isolates the mixing operation without applying symmetric normalization: with scalar features 2,1,42,1,4, node one averages itself and both neighbors to 7/37/3. Multiplying by 0.50.5 and applying ReLU gives about 1.171.17. A second node connected only to node one averages to 1.51.5 and updates to 0.750.75. The example isolates the information-mixing idea.

5 · State the graph assumptions

This symmetric construction has its usual spectral interpretation for an undirected graph with nonnegative edge weights. Directed graphs need an explicit direction convention and normalization choice. Adding self-loops changes degrees and therefore all normalization weights, not only the diagonal entries.

Equivariance versus invariance

For node predictions, relabeling nodes should relabel outputs in the same way: permutation equivariance . For a single graph-level output, a symmetric readout such as summing final node embeddings produces permutation invariance .

Those are different requirements. A graph classifier that changes prediction merely because rows were reordered violates the intended invariance. A node classifier must still preserve which output belongs to which node.

Step by step Prove node equivariance and graph-level invariance

Setting. Let node features be HH and symmetrically normalized adjacency be SS . A layer is F(H,A)=σ(SHW)F(H,A)=\sigma(SHW) with shared weights WW and elementwise σ\sigma . Let PP be a permutation matrix relabeling nodes.

1 · Relabel both features and relationships

The relabeled features are H′=PHH'=PH and adjacency A′=PAP⊤A'=PAP^\top . Since PIP⊤=IPIP^\top=I , self-loop adjacency becomes Ã′=PÃP⊤\tilde A'=P\tilde AP^\top . Row sums reorder the degree entries, so D̃′=PD̃P⊤\tilde D'=P\tilde DP^\top .

2 · Relabel normalization consistently

For a positive diagonal degree matrix, taking inverse square roots reorders the same diagonal values:

(D̃′)−1/2=PD̃−1/2P⊤.(\tilde D')^{-1/2}=P\tilde D^{-1/2}P^\top.

Substituting into the normalized adjacency and canceling P⊤P=IP^\top P=I gives S′=PSP⊤S'=PSP^\top .

3 · Propagate and use shared elementwise operations

F(H′,A′)=σ(S′H′W)=σ(PSP⊤PHW)=σ(PSHW).F(H',A')=\sigma(S'H'W)=\sigma(PSP^\top PHW)=\sigma(PSHW).

A row permutation commutes with the shared elementwise nonlinearity, so

F(H′,A′)=Pσ(SHW)=PF(H,A).F(H',A')=P\sigma(SHW)=PF(H,A).

Outputs reorder with nodes: equivariance. Permuting features without also permuting adjacency would describe a different graph and would not satisfy this calculation.

4 · Pool to get an invariant graph representation

A sum readout is g=𝟏⊤H(L)g=\mathbf1^\top H^{(L)} . Under relabeling,

g′=𝟏⊤PH(L)=𝟏⊤H(L)=g,g'=\mathbf1^\top PH^{(L)}=\mathbf1^\top H^{(L)}=g,

because 𝟏⊤P=𝟏⊤\mathbf1^\top P=\mathbf1^\top . Mean and componentwise maximum pooling are also invariant to ordering. Shared edge scoring and permutation-symmetric aggregation preserve analogous properties in message-passing models.

5 · Keep symmetry distinct from expressiveness

A model can obey the correct permutation symmetry and still fail to distinguish particular graph structures or long-range relationships. Equivariance is a consistency property under relabeling, not a universal expressiveness guarantee.

Depth brings both reach and bottlenecks

After LL local message-passing layers without extra long-range edges, information can travel at most LL graph hops. Repeated propagation can suppress discriminative node directions: over-smoothing . In a connected undirected graph, pure row-normalized diffusion approaches a common stationary average, whereas symmetric normalization approaches a degree-weighted dominant subspace. Raw node values need not all become equal; the expandable calculation below makes the distinction explicit.

Over-squashing is different: information from a rapidly growing distant neighborhood must pass through narrow graph bottlenecks into a fixed-width vector. Representations can remain distinct while still failing to convey enough distant information.

Distinguish homophily—connected nodes being similar—from heterophily, where neighboring nodes can differ systematically. Naive averaging can be inappropriate when the relation means opposition, complementarity, or a different role rather than similarity.

Step by step Derive the degree-weighted diffusion limit behind oversmoothing

Setting. Consider repeated linear propagation on a connected undirected nonnegative graph with self-loops: H(L)=SLH(0)H^{(L)}=S^LH^{(0)} , where S=D̃−1/2ÃD̃−1/2S=\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2} . Assume the associated random walk is irreducible and aperiodic, so the eigenvalue one is simple and all other eigenvalues have absolute value below one.

1 · Find the leading eigenvector

Let v=D̃1/2𝟏v=\tilde D^{1/2}\mathbf1 , whose entries are d̃i\sqrt{\tilde d_i} . Then

Sv=D̃−1/2ÃD̃−1/2D̃1/2𝟏=D̃−1/2Ã𝟏=D̃−1/2D̃𝟏=v.Sv=\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde D^{1/2}\mathbf1 =\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde A\mathbf1 =\tilde D^{-1/2}\tilde D\mathbf1=v.

Normalize it to u=v/∑id̃iu=v/\sqrt{\sum_i\tilde d_i} .

2 · Expand powers in an orthonormal eigenbasis

Symmetry gives S=UΛU⊤S=U\Lambda U^\top , with first column uu and λ1=1\lambda_1=1 . Thus

SL=uu⊤+∑j=2nλjLujuj⊤.S^L=uu^\top+\sum_{j=2}^n\lambda_j^L u_ju_j^\top.

Because |λj|<1|\lambda_j|<1 for j≥2j\ge2 , each remaining power tends to zero.

3 · Write the limit explicitly

H(L)→uu⊤H(0).H^{(L)}\longrightarrow uu^\top H^{(0)}.

For one scalar feature, the limiting value at node ii is

hi(∞)=d̃i∑jd̃j∑jd̃jhj(0).h_i^{(\infty)}=\frac{\sqrt{\tilde d_i}}{\sum_j\tilde d_j} \sum_j\sqrt{\tilde d_j}\,h_j^{(0)}.

The result lies in a one-dimensional node subspace. Nodes need not have identical raw values when their degrees differ.

4 · Compare row-normalized diffusion

For R=D̃−1ÃR=\tilde D^{-1}\tilde A , the limiting rows are identical stationary-distribution averages:

RL→𝟏π⊤,πi=d̃i∑jd̃j.R^L\longrightarrow\mathbf1\pi^\top,\qquad \pi_i=\frac{\tilde d_i}{\sum_j\tilde d_j}.

This is why “all nodes approach a common average” is literal for row-normalized connected aperiodic diffusion, but needs a degree-weighted qualification for symmetric normalization.

5 · Separate this mechanism from a full GNN theorem

Learned transformations, nonlinearities, skip connections, disconnected components, and varying layer operators alter the dynamics. A raw feature-variance curve need not decrease monotonically under every such model. This simplified limit explains how repeated propagation can erase discriminative directions; over-squashing is a different limitation involving compression of many distant messages through narrow routes.

Graph attention learns the neighbor weights

A GAT transforms node features zi=Whiz_i=Wh_i and computes scores such as

eij=LeakyReLU(a⊤[zi‖zj]),e_{ij}=\operatorname{LeakyReLU}(a^\top[z_i\Vert z_j]),
αij=exp(eij)∑k∈𝒩(i)∪{i}exp(eik),h′i=σ(∑j∈𝒩(i)∪{i}αijzj).\alpha_{ij}=\frac{\exp(e_{ij})}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal N(i)\cup\{i\}}\exp(e_{ik})},\qquad h'_i=\sigma\left(\sum_{j\in\mathcal N(i)\cup\{i\}}\alpha_{ij}z_j\right).

The weights are normalized separately for each center node’s allowed neighborhood. For example, logits (0.81,1.85,0,1.06)(0.81,1.85,0,1.06) give approximately (0.18,0.51,0.08,0.23)(0.18,0.51,0.08,0.23). The largest weight is the largest scalar mixing coefficient, not necessarily the largest contribution in magnitude or the strongest effect on the final prediction: the transformed value vectors and later layers also matter.

Multiple heads can be concatenated in intermediate layers or averaged at a final layer. Unlike standard dense Transformer attention, a GAT’s interaction set is normally constrained by graph edges. Learned weights do not automatically give arbitrary long-range access.

mi=∑j∈𝒩iαijzj.m_i=\sum_{j\in\mathcal N_i}\alpha_{ij}z_j.

Normalization is separate for each center node’s permitted neighborhood. A high score for one edge changes the relative weights of all edges in that same neighborhood.

3 · Differentiate with respect to one edge score

The softmax derivative is ∂αij/∂eir=αij(𝟏j=r−αir)\partial\alpha_{ij}/\partial e_{ir}=\alpha_{ij}(\mathbf1_{j=r}-\alpha_{ir}) . Holding the transformed values fixed,

∂mi∂eir=∑jzjαij(𝟏j=r−αir)=αir(zr−mi).\frac{\partial m_i}{\partial e_{ir}} =\sum_jz_j\alpha_{ij}(\mathbf1_{j=r}-\alpha_{ir}) =\alpha_{ir}(z_r-m_i).

Increasing a score pulls the mixture toward that neighbor’s transformed vector, not toward its raw features.

4 · Contract with a downstream gradient

If gi=∇miLg_i=\nabla_{m_i}L , then

∂L∂eir=αirgi⊤(zr−mi).\frac{\partial L}{\partial e_{ir}}=\alpha_{ir}\,g_i^\top(z_r-m_i).

Continue through the LeakyReLU score derivative and its affine transform. A node feature also contributes through the value path zjz_j , so its total gradient combines scoring and value routes, as in ordinary attention.

5 · Avoid interpreting one coefficient as the whole explanation

A coefficient measures a normalized mixing weight at one layer/head. Its predictive effect also depends on the value vector, nonlinearities, other heads, and downstream paths. A large coefficient is therefore neither a causal effect estimate nor a complete measure of feature importance.

Attention is not a causal explanation

A large attention coefficient is not a complete measure of importance or causality. The transformed values, residual paths, other heads, and downstream nonlinearities also influence the prediction. Attention visualizations describe part of the computation, not its entire explanation.

Evaluate the actual graph task

Node, link, and graph-level prediction require different output and split designs. Random transductive node splits, temporally ordered links, and unseen-graph evaluation answer different questions. Graph construction itself can introduce unavailable future information.

Check your understanding What is the difference between over-smoothing and over-squashing?

Over-smoothing makes node representations too similar after repeated propagation. Over-squashing compresses information from many distant nodes through narrow graph pathways into fixed-size vectors. Solving one does not necessarily solve the other.
