Curvature, conditioning, and preconditioning

The Hessian describes how the gradient changes across parameter space. Curvature and conditioning explain why a single learning rate can be safe in one direction but inefficient in another.

The gradient describes slope, not the shape of the valley

A second-order local expansion is

J(θ+p)≈J(θ)+g⊤p+12p⊤Hp,J(\theta+p)\approx J(\theta)+g^\top p+\frac12p^\top Hp,

where g=∇J(θ)g=\nabla J(\theta) and H=∇2J(θ)H=\nabla^2J(\theta) is the Hessian. The gradient tells how the objective changes to first order. The Hessian describes how that gradient changes as parameters move.

Minimizing the local quadratic approximation gives the Newton equation

Hp=−g,p=−H−1gHp=-g,\qquad p=-H^{-1}g

when the inverse and suitable curvature conditions make this expression meaningful. This is a direction derived from a quadratic model, not simply a longer negative-gradient step.

Step by step Derive the Newton system and its descent condition

Setting. JJ is twice differentiable. At the current point, let g=∇Jg=\nabla J and H=∇2JH=\nabla^2J . Assume HH is symmetric, as holds for continuous second derivatives.

1 · Write the quadratic approximation

q(p)=J(θ)+g⊤p+12p⊤Hp.q(p)=J(\theta)+g^\top p+\frac12p^\top Hp.

The approximation concerns displacement pp while J(θ),g,HJ(\theta),g,H are fixed.

2 · Differentiate the quadratic term by entries

12p⊤Hp=12∑i,jpiHijpj.\frac12p^\top Hp=\frac12\sum_{i,j}p_iH_{ij}p_j.

For coordinate pkp_k , differentiate occurrences in both factors:

∂∂pk12p⊤Hp=12∑jHkjpj+12∑ipiHik=(Hp)k.\frac{\partial}{\partial p_k}\frac12p^\top Hp =\frac12\sum_jH_{kj}p_j+\frac12\sum_i p_iH_{ik}=(Hp)_k.

Symmetry equates the two sums.

3 · Set the quadratic-model gradient to zero

The derivative of g⊤pg^\top p is gg , giving g+Hp=0g+Hp=0 . If HH is positive definite, qq has a unique minimum and

Hp=−g.Hp=-g.

Writing p=−H−1gp=-H^{-1}g describes the solution; numerical implementations should normally solve the system rather than explicitly form the inverse.

4 · Check whether the direction descends

g⊤p=−g⊤H−1g<0when g≠0 and H≻0.g^\top p=-g^\top H^{-1}g<0\quad\text{when }g\ne0\text{ and }H\succ0.

An invertible but indefinite Hessian is not enough. For J(w)=−w2/2J(w)=-w^2/2 at w=1w=1 , g=−1g=-1 , H=−1H=-1 , and Newton gives p=−1p=-1 , moving to the maximum at zero and increasing the objective from −1/2-1/2 to zero.

5 · Interpret damping

Replacing HH by H+λIH+\lambda I with sufficient λ>0\lambda>0 can make the local system positive definite, producing a descent direction. A line search may still be needed because the quadratic is only local. Curvature information and safe step length are separate requirements.

Why conditioning slows gradient descent

For a positive-definite quadratic with Hessian AA , the condition number is

κ(A)=λmax(A)λmin(A).\kappa(A)=\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A)}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}.

When it is large, some directions are much steeper than others. A step size safe for the steep direction can make little progress along the shallow direction. The contour ellipses become elongated, producing the familiar zig-zagging picture.

In an eigenvector direction with eigenvalue λj\lambda_j, gradient descent multiplies the error by 1−ηλj1-\eta\lambda_j each step. Stability requires a rate compatible with the largest eigenvalue, while the smallest eigenvalue can then have a contraction factor very close to one. The same global rate must satisfy both demands.

A preconditioner changes the geometry of the step. It tries to make unequal directions more comparable rather than merely shrink the same unhelpful motion.

Step by step Derive gradient-descent contraction in each eigen-direction

Setting. f(x)=12x⊤Ax−b⊤xf(x)=\tfrac12x^\top Ax-b^\top x with symmetric positive-definite AA . Let x*=A−1bx_*=A^{-1}b and et=xt−x*e_t=x_t-x_* .

Since Ax*=bAx_*=b ,

xt+1=xt−η(Axt−b),et+1=et−ηAet=(I−ηA)et.x_{t+1}=x_t-\eta(Ax_t-b),\qquad e_{t+1}=e_t-\eta Ae_t=(I-\eta A)e_t.

Write A=QΛQ⊤A=Q\Lambda Q^\top , where Q⊤Q=IQ^\top Q=I and Λ\Lambda has positive eigenvalues λj\lambda_j . Let ct=Q⊤etc_t=Q^\top e_t . Then

ct+1=Q⊤(I−ηA)Qct=(I−ηΛ)ct.c_{t+1}=Q^\top(I-\eta A)Qc_t=(I-\eta\Lambda)c_t.

Thus ct+1,j=(1−ηλj)ct,jc_{t+1,j}=(1-\eta\lambda_j)c_{t,j} and ct,j=(1−ηλj)tc0,jc_{t,j}=(1-\eta\lambda_j)^t c_{0,j} .

3 · Require contraction in every direction

The condition |1−ηλj|<1|1-\eta\lambda_j|<1 is equivalent to 0<η<2/λj0<\eta<2/\lambda_j . Satisfying every coordinate requires

0<η<2/λmax.0<\eta<2/\lambda_{\max}.

The maximum one-step contraction factor is maxj|1−ηλj|\max_j|1-\eta\lambda_j| .

4 · Balance the extreme eigenvalues

The best constant rate for this worst-case factor equates the two endpoint magnitudes:

1−ηλmin=−(1−ηλmax).1-\eta\lambda_{\min}=-(1-\eta\lambda_{\max}).

Therefore η*=2/(λmax+λmin)\eta_*=2/(\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}) , and the factor is

λmax−λminλmax+λmin=κ−1κ+1.\frac{\lambda_{\max}-\lambda_{\min}}{\lambda_{\max}+\lambda_{\min}}=\frac{\kappa-1}{\kappa+1}.

For κ=100\kappa=100 , the factor is 99/101≈0.980299/101\approx0.9802 ; many contractions are required.

With positive-definite PP , the error update is (I−ηPA)et(I-\eta PA)e_t . Set zt=P−1/2etz_t=P^{-1/2}e_t . Then

zt+1=[I−ηP1/2AP1/2]zt.z_{t+1}=[I-\eta P^{1/2}AP^{1/2}]z_t.

The relevant symmetric matrix is P1/2AP1/2P^{1/2}AP^{1/2} . A good preconditioner improves its spectrum. If P=A−1P=A^{-1} , this matrix is II , and a unit-rate step solves this exact quadratic in one iteration; general nonlinear objectives need not do so.

Diagonal adaptation versus full curvature

AdaGrad, RMSProp, and Adam estimate coordinate scales from gradient history. These estimates are inexpensive and compatible with noisy mini-batches. But they are diagonal in the current parameter coordinates.

The Hessian contains off-diagonal terms representing cross-coordinate interactions. Those terms are essential in a rotated valley: progress along one raw coordinate may need a coordinated change in another. A full Newton or quasi-Newton approximation can represent that coupling; independent coordinate rescaling generally cannot.

This does not make second-order methods universally preferable. It explains what additional information they try to exploit and why that information is expensive.

Cost and the neural-network setting

A dense Hessian for dd parameters has d2d^2 entries. Large networks make storage, estimation, and matrix solution costly. Moreover, neural losses are generally nonconvex; a local Hessian need not be positive definite, so the ideal convex-quadratic picture is not automatically a safe descent procedure.

Conjugate Gradient can solve certain positive-definite linear systems using matrix–vector products without explicitly inverting a matrix. BFGS estimates curvature from parameter and gradient changes. L-BFGS keeps only a limited history. These methods approach the cost problem in different ways.

Hessian–vector products expose curvature along a chosen direction without storing the full matrix. The function-transform examples below demonstrate this calculation with autograd.

Better scaling can help before optimization starts

The linear model ŷ=θ1x1+θ2x2\hat y=\theta_1x_1+\theta_2x_2 illustrates the effect of input units. If one feature is numerically a thousand times larger, its corresponding gradient can also be on a very different scale. Standardization can improve conditioning before an adaptive optimizer is involved.

Initialization, normalization layers, and residual architecture also influence the optimization geometry. Optimizer choice is therefore not independent of representation design. A useful diagnosis asks whether the hard geometry is intrinsic to the task or partly created by the way the data and model are parameterized.

Check your understanding What can a full curvature model do that a diagonal adaptive optimizer cannot?

It can represent interactions between coordinates and rotate as well as rescale a search direction. A diagonal preconditioner only rescales along the current coordinate axes, so it cannot generally correct an arbitrarily rotated ill-conditioned valley.
