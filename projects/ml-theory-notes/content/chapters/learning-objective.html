The model is a function; training chooses the function

Write a predictor as ŷ=fθ(x)\hat y=f_\theta(x) . The input xx is an observed example, the output ŷ\hat y is a prediction, and θ\theta collects the parameters that training can change. In a dense neural network those parameters are primarily weight matrices and bias vectors. A forward pass evaluates this function at the current parameter values; it does not itself learn anything.

A loss ℓ(y,fθ(x))\ell(y,f_\theta(x)) says which prediction errors matter. For NN examples, the empirical objective in the optimization guide is

J(θ)=1N∑i=1Nℓ(yi,fθ(xi)),θ*∈argminθJ(θ).J(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ell(y_i,f_\theta(x_i)),\qquad \theta^*\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\theta}J(\theta).

“Empirical” means that the average is over the observed training sample. The network is not given the true relationship everywhere in the input space. It must infer a useful function from finitely many examples. That is why a procedure can become very good at its training objective without becoming equally good at the task we actually care about.

Step by step From independent observations to empirical risk

Setting. Condition on the observed inputs x1,…,xNx_1,\ldots,x_N . Assume the targets are conditionally independent under a model pθ(y∣x)p_\theta(y\mid x) . All logarithms here are natural logarithms.

1 · Write the probability of the complete target sample

Conditional independence makes the joint likelihood a product:

pθ(y1,…,yN∣x1,…,xN)=∏i=1Npθ(yi∣xi).p_\theta(y_1,\ldots,y_N\mid x_1,\ldots,x_N)=\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i).

This is a modeling assumption, not an identity for dependent observations. Sequence models use a conditional factorization instead.

2 · Take a logarithm

The logarithm is increasing, so maximizing the likelihood is equivalent to maximizing its logarithm. Applying log(ab)=loga+logb\log(ab)=\log a+\log b repeatedly gives

log∏i=1Npθ(yi∣xi)=∑i=1Nlogpθ(yi∣xi).\log\prod_{i=1}^N p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)=\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i).

3 · Turn maximization into minimization

Define the per-observation negative log-likelihood ℓi(θ)=−logpθ(yi∣xi)\ell_i(\theta)=-\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i) . Multiplication by the negative constant −1/N-1/N reverses maximization to minimization but does not change which parameter values optimize it:

J(θ)=−1N∑i=1Nlogpθ(yi∣xi)=1N∑i=1Nℓi(θ).J(\theta)=-\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\log p_\theta(y_i\mid x_i)=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\ell_i(\theta).

Thus mean squared error and cross entropy can arise from different likelihood assumptions, rather than being unrelated recipes. Not every useful loss needs a likelihood interpretation.

4 · Differentiate the finite sum

For parameter coordinate θj\theta_j , constants leave derivatives and finite sums differentiate term by term:

∂J∂θj=1N∑i=1N∂ℓi∂θj.\frac{\partial J}{\partial\theta_j}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\frac{\partial\ell_i}{\partial\theta_j}.

Collect these coordinates into ∇θJ\nabla_\theta J . The equality requires derivatives at the evaluation point; nonsmooth functions need a specified subgradient or software convention.

5 · Separate a population quantity from its estimate

The prediction objective for a fresh pair (X,Y)(X,Y) is R(θ)=𝔼[ℓ(Y,fθ(X))]R(\theta)=\mathbb E[\ell(Y,f_\theta(X))] . For independent identically distributed observations and a fixed θ\theta chosen independently of them,

𝔼[J(θ)]=1N∑i𝔼[ℓi(θ)]=R(θ).\mathbb E[J(\theta)]=\frac1N\sum_i\mathbb E[\ell_i(\theta)]=R(\theta).

After fitting θ̂\hat\theta on the same data, θ̂\hat\theta is no longer independent of those losses. The fixed-parameter equality does not establish that J(θ̂)J(\hat\theta) is an unbiased estimate of its fresh-data risk. That is why evaluation must be separated from fitting.

Four choices that should not be confused

The architecture determines the family of functions available: an affine model, a composition of nonlinear layers, a recurrent state transition, or a graph-based computation. The loss defines what is rewarded. Backpropagation computes the sensitivity of that loss to the parameters. The optimizer turns those sensitivities into a parameter update.

For instance, a nonlinear regressor and a linear regressor can minimize the same squared-error loss while expressing very different functions. Two identical networks can use SGD and Adam and therefore follow different paths through parameter space. Neither “Adam” nor “cross entropy” is an architecture.

The useful sequence is

x→fθ(x)→ℓ→∇θJ→θnew.x\ \longrightarrow\ f_\theta(x)\ \longrightarrow\ \ell\ \longrightarrow\ \nabla_\theta J\ \longrightarrow\ \theta_{\mathrm{new}}.

The derivative tells us what a small local change would do. It is not a certificate that the model has discovered a causal relationship, nor that the chosen change improves unseen-data performance.

Fitting observed data versus learning stable structure

The regularization guide distinguishes stable signal from sampling noise, idiosyncratic outliers, nuisance variation, and spurious interactions. A flexible model can lower empirical loss by fitting all of them. Regularization deliberately makes some solutions harder to obtain:

θ̂∈argminθ[J(θ)+λΩ(θ)].\hat\theta\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_\theta\left[J(\theta)+\lambda\Omega(\theta)\right].

Here Ω\Omega is a penalty and λ\lambda controls its strength. But the guide’s definition is broader than penalties. Augmentation changes the inputs seen during training; early stopping restricts how far optimization proceeds; dropout changes the effective network during a training pass; ensembles combine predictions. These methods intervene at different stages of the learning process.

The question is therefore not simply “How can I minimize the training loss faster?” It is also “Which solutions and procedures are likely to retain useful behavior outside the training sample?” The bias–variance chapter makes that distinction mathematical.

Step by step Why a prior becomes a regularization penalty

Setting. Let DD be the training data and p(θ)p(\theta) a prior density. Maximum a posteriori estimation selects a posterior mode , not a posterior mean.

1 · Start from Bayes’ rule

p(θ∣D)=p(D∣θ)p(θ)p(D).p(\theta\mid D)=\frac{p(D\mid\theta)p(\theta)}{p(D)}.

When optimizing over θ\theta , p(D)p(D) is constant. Taking a negative logarithm therefore gives the same optimizer as

−logp(D∣θ)−logp(θ).-\log p(D\mid\theta)-\log p(\theta).

2 · Expand a Gaussian observation model

Suppose yi∣xi,β∼𝒩(xi⊤β,σ2)y_i\mid x_i,\beta\sim\mathcal N(x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2) with known σ2>0\sigma^2>0 . Its density is

12πσ2exp[−(yi−xi⊤β)22σ2].\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\!\left[-\frac{(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].

Taking the negative log and summing gives

N2log(2πσ2)+12σ2∥y−Xβ∥2.\frac N2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)+\frac1{2\sigma^2}\|y-X\beta\|^2.

The first term does not depend on β\beta and may be dropped only for this optimization .

3 · Expand a Gaussian prior

Independent βj∼𝒩(0,τ2)\beta_j\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^2) give

−logp(β)=∥β∥22τ2+constant.-\log p(\beta)=\frac{\|\beta\|^2}{2\tau^2}+\text{constant}.

Multiply the remaining objective by σ2\sigma^2 to obtain

12∥y−Xβ∥2+σ22τ2∥β∥2.\frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{2\tau^2}\|\beta\|^2.

This is Ridge with λ=σ2/τ2\lambda=\sigma^2/\tau^2 for the half-summed-loss convention. With a mean loss, the matching coefficient is σ2/(Nτ2)\sigma^2/(N\tau^2) .

4 · Replace the prior with a Laplace density

An independent Laplace prior with scale b>0b>0 has p(βj)=(2b)−1exp(−|βj|/b)p(\beta_j)=(2b)^{-1}\exp(-|\beta_j|/b) . Hence

−logp(β)=1b∑j|βj|+constant.-\log p(\beta)=\frac1b\sum_j|\beta_j|+\text{constant}.

Using the same likelihood and multiplying by σ2\sigma^2 yields

12∥y−Xβ∥2+σ2b∥β∥1.\frac12\|y-X\beta\|^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{b}\|\beta\|_1.

This is the LASSO objective. A continuous prior assigning zero probability to the exact event βj=0\beta_j=0 can nevertheless have a posterior mode at zero; density and point probability are different quantities.

5 · Keep the interpretation conditional

A penalty can be useful without believing that the stated prior is literally the data-generating distribution. Different units, noise scales, and loss reductions change the implied prior strength. An unpenalized intercept must be handled separately from the coefficient prior.

A practical case from the regularization guide

Consider its hypothetical dataset with 500 observations, 200 standardized predictors, correlated groups, and approximately 25 genuinely informative variables. A flexible network may represent the signal well but also fit unstable interactions. Ridge would stabilize coefficient estimates; LASSO could remove variables; Elastic Net could combine selection with correlated-feature stability. A neural network could instead combine modest penalties, valid augmentation, and early stopping.

These are not interchangeable recipes with a guaranteed winner. They impose different preferences. The hypothetical case illustrates how to match the intervention to the suspected failure rather than add complexity by default.

What to establish before interpreting a result

A training measurement describes how well the fitted function explains observations used for learning. A validation measurement guides choices such as regularization strength, architecture, or stopping time. Once a dataset repeatedly influences those choices, it participates in model selection. The guides explicitly warn against using the final test set for those decisions and against breaking chronology in temporal applications.

For now, keep one distinction in mind: capacity determines what could be represented; optimization determines what is reached; generalization determines how well it transfers to unseen data. The next chapters develop the first two before returning to generalization in detail.

Check your understanding Why can a lower training loss give a worse model?

The training objective measures fit to the observed sample. Additional flexibility or optimization can fit accidental sample structure, increasing error on unseen observations. Optimization success and generalization are distinct; validation and regularization address the latter.
